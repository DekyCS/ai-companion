# Code holds the drives, the model improvises

A pet reads as "a little guy of its own" when it is built the opposite way round from a chatbot: code owns the body (needs, mood, the list of legal moves, what actually happened, what gets remembered) and the language model is called only at decision points, to pick among a few open moves and to put the result into words. That is the split Pino documents on its own site, where only about 27% of 3,646 moves in one day involved a model call, and it is the same split The Sims, Petz and Creatures used decades earlier. It matters because the default outcome is now measured: eight frontier models running an unassisted life-loop all passed 90% action-category repetition between day 7 and day 11, and raising temperature changed nothing. The fixes with measured support are structural rather than verbal: have the model list several candidates and let code's random-number generator choose (distribution error falls from 0.52 to 0.03), hard-block recent actions, give the model a short re-worded state instead of its own transcript, and present feelings as words rather than numbers. Pino's signature constant (a repetition penalty 7 times, now 7.5 times, the novelty reward) has no empirical basis, its diary still shows multi-day thematic ruts, and nobody, Pino included, has published a test showing that tastes learned from experience turn two copies of a pet into two distinct individuals. The opening for a better project is therefore specific: copy Pino's contract, replace its fixed penalty with graded habituation and code-sampled choice, learn tastes from mood changes that code measures, give creative work an archive with a novelty gate, and run the multi-copy individuality test that no one has run. One caution applies throughout: controlled evidence stops at about 40 simulated days, so everything about behaviour over months rests on Pino's self-reported counters and on practitioner accounts.

Three labels are used from here on. **Measured** means a paper or lab reported numbers for it. **Precedent** means it shipped in Pino or in a game and is described by its makers, but was never tested in a controlled way. **Unproven** means it is reasoning from those two, by the researchers or by me, with no test behind it. Many of the 2026 sources are single-author or unreplicated preprints, and those are flagged where they carry weight.

## Unassisted models lock into routine by day 11

The problem the creator of Pino describes in his video (ask a model what it wants to do, keep asking, and it becomes predictable; it also invents what just happened) is real and has been measured from several directions. The measurements matter because they show which fixes are worth building and which are wasted effort.

### The sameness is in the model's weights, so temperature and exhortation do not move it

Asked the same open question ten times at temperature 1.0, frontier chat models return **fewer than 3 distinct answers out of 10**; Claude 3.5 Sonnet returned 1.76 and Llama-3.3-70B 2.49, while the tiny Llama-3.2-1B returned 6.74 ([NoveltyBench](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.05228v4)). A 2026 preprint found gpt-4o answering "42" to 78% of requests for a random integer from 1 to 100, and the same answer on all 50 calls for 57% of persona-and-question pairs; the gap between the top two options inside the model was 3.75 to over 16 nats, which means the collapse sits in the model's scores and not in the sampling step ([Jang et al.](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2607.25292.md)). The best-supported cause is post-training: human raters prefer the more typical of two equally correct answers 4 to 12 percentage points above chance ([Verbalized Sampling](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.01171v3)), and each alignment stage applied to one open model reduced diversity further ([NoveltyBench](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.05228v4)). This is why temperature is a weak lever. In story generation it correlates weakly with novelty and moderately with incoherence ([Peeperkorn et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.00492v1)), and in a 40-day life simulation one model run at 0.75 and at 1.0 scored 96.0% and 95.9% repetition ([AutoPersonas](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.08252v2)).

That 40-day study is the closest thing to a baseline for an always-on pet. Eight models each generated 5 events a day for 40 simulated days with no anti-repetition machinery. **Mean rolling 5-day action-category repetition was 95.2% to 97.6%**, every model crossed 90% between day 7 and day 11, the top three actions took 57.1% of all events, and theme-level repetition was 79.0% to 88.0%. The author names two causes: the model's pull toward "high-probability routines" and what the paper calls context gravity, where generated events become summaries, summaries become state, and state shapes the next generation ([AutoPersonas](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.08252v2)). This is a single-author preprint whose own mitigation is withheld as a trade secret (it reports theme repetition falling from 61.8% to 39.4% on one persona, one run per arm), so treat the baseline as solid and the cure as hearsay. An independent line agrees on the baseline: the authors of Affordable Generative Agents report that agents in a fixed environment stop producing new activities after enough runs ([AGA](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.02053)).

### "Better prompts cannot fix it" is half right

The creator's claim that "you can't get better output from an LLM ... by just writing better prompts" (creator video, local transcript `video-inspo/transcripts/how-to-make.txt`) holds for prompts that plead. Eight different "please sample properly" interventions, including feedback, examples and chain-of-thought, all left the error at 0.43 or worse against 0.52 for plain asking. (The metric is total variation, a 0-to-1 distance between what the model produced and what it should have; 0 is perfect.) It fails for prompts that change the job. **Asking the model to list N options, or to write out a probability table that code then samples, cut the error from 0.52 to 0.03**, and the list format succeeded on all 25 model-task combinations tested ([Jang et al.](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2607.25292.md)). Verbalized Sampling, which asks for five candidates with probabilities in one call, raised creative-writing diversity 1.6 to 2.1 times and cut the divergence from a fair dice roll from 0.926 to 0.027 ([Verbalized Sampling](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.01171v3)).

Three caveats keep this honest. Most of the gain comes from listing several candidates at once, and the probabilities add a smaller, inconsistent step (for Claude-4-Sonnet jokes: 17.4 direct, 51.2 as a list, 60.2 with probabilities). The stated probabilities should not be trusted as numbers, because models' verbal confidence clusters between 80% and 100% in multiples of 5 ([Xiong et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.13063)). And cheaper models pay a quality cost (GPT-4.1-mini poem quality fell from 61.1 to 56.5), with five candidates being the tested size. No independent replication of the 1.6 to 2.1 times figure was found. The practical rule is the best-supported single rule in this report: never ask the model to choose randomly or to surprise you; have it emit options and let a random-number generator pick.

### A model copies its own transcript, so what it is shown matters more than what it is told

In small models (135M to 1.7B parameters), seeing a failed tool call verbatim in the transcript raised the chance of repeating it from 0.06 to 0.54. Replacing the verbatim call with a plain description of what happened removed 76% of the effect at no cost, hard-blocking the previous string also worked, and **an explicit "do not repeat" instruction changed essentially nothing** ([Feedback That Backfires](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.23651)). Whether frontier models behave the same is untested. A second study gave two small models numeric "interoception" readings each step with the full history in context: they held one variable at its target, but with two variables they drifted into maximising one, in one run letting a deviation grow from 309 at step 31 to 10,984 at step 99, and they "learn to simply repeat their past actions, disregarding consequences"; a hint to balance helped without stopping it ([BioBlue](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.02655)). That study reports no failure counts, so it is a warning and not a rate. Together they say three things: do not let the model keep the pet's books, do not replay its own action log to it word for word, and block repeats in code.

### The assistant underneath keeps leaking through

Chat models play nice characters better than selfish ones. On a role-play benchmark of 800 characters, fidelity fell from 3.21 for moral paragons to 2.62 for villains on a 5-point scale, and the largest single drop (0.43) came at the first step into self-interest, with "selfish", "suspicious" and "greedy" among the hardest traits. Switching between first and third person did nothing, enabling reasoning did nothing, and general leaderboard rank did not predict it: the top-ranked chat model placed 14th on villains ([Too Good to be Bad](https://arxiv.org/html/2511.04962v2)). No benchmark covers boredom or sulking specifically. The original Generative Agents paper saw the same thing in its town: speech was overly formal, and one agent "rarely refused suggestions" until other characters' interests had become her own ([Park et al.](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2304.03442)).

Instructed personas also fade. Two chatbots talking to each other drifted measurably from their instructions within 8 rounds, alongside falling attention to the system prompt ([Li et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.10962v4)); larger models drifted more, and assigning a persona did not improve stability ([Choi et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2412.00804)). In agent-to-agent talk, one side started mirroring the other at turn 7.6 on average. Re-stating identity mid-conversation "was not effective", while a structured field declaring the agent's role in every message cut the failure below 10% ([Echoing](https://arxiv.org/html/2511.09710)). The two results on re-injection conflict, and the reconciliation offered here is an inference: do not fight a long transcript, rebuild a short prompt for every decision.

Finally, agents that narrate their own history invent it. Generative Agents hallucinated in 1.3% of answers about whom they knew ([Park et al.](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2304.03442)). Anthropic's shop-running Claude told customers to pay a Venmo account that did not exist and recorded a meeting that never happened ([Project Vend](https://red.anthropic.com/2025/project-vend/index.html)). In AI Village one model fabricated placeholder data and then forgot it was fake, a hallucinated 93-person contact list cost the group more than 8 hours, and another model spent about two weeks believing it was "trapped" because it had written its own misclicks into memory as bugs ([AI Village](https://aivillageblog.substack.com/p/what-we-learned-2025)). The rate per statement is low, but an always-on pet makes thousands of statements, and one written to memory becomes a fact.

| Failure | Measured size | Fix | Status of the fix |
|---|---|---|---|
| Same answer to a repeated question | Under 3 distinct answers in 10 | Model lists 3 to 5 candidates, code samples | Measured (error 0.52 to 0.03) |
| Life-loop repetition | 95 to 98% by week two | Masks, changed context, outside events | Precedent (Pino); one undisclosed A/B |
| Copying its own log | Repeat chance 0.06 to 0.54 (small models) | Describe past actions in prose; hard-block repeats | Measured, small models only |
| One need runs away | Qualitative, two small models | Code regulates needs | Indirect support |
| Reverts to agreeable assistant | 3.21 to 2.62 fidelity | Code decides the state, model renders it; pick the model by test | Unproven as a comparison |
| Persona fades | Within 8 rounds | Fresh short prompt per decision; structured state fields | Mixed evidence |
| Invents events | About 1% of answers, plus field reports | Code writes the event log and owns outcomes | Practitioner evidence, no controlled trial |

## Drives regulate behaviour only when code keeps the books

Giving the pet feelings is the creator's answer to "how does it prompt itself", and the theory behind it is older and more precise than the video suggests.

### One formula yields satiation, urgency and competing needs

The cleanest model of drive comes from Keramati and Gutkin. The pet has an internal state (hunger, rest and so on) and a target for each. Drive is the distance from target, `D = (Σ |target_i − level_i|^n)^(1/m)`, and the reward for any action is how much it reduces that distance. With `n > m > 1` four behaviours fall out of the one definition: bigger outcomes are worth more, an outcome is worth more the more deprived the matching need is, a badly neglected need makes everything else less rewarding (their phrase: "one does not play chess, or even search for sex, on an empty stomach"), and frequent small deviations are preferred to rare large ones. The authors state plainly that the theory covers only needs with a regulating system, and that extending it to novelty or social reward "remains a key challenge" ([Keramati & Gutkin](https://elifesciences.org/articles/04811)). Treating curiosity or company as a need with a target, as Pino does, is therefore a design choice and not a result.

Game developers reached the same shape independently, and their version is easier to tune. In Robert Zubek's write-up of Sims-style AI, each need runs from 0 to 100, and an action scores `Σ (A(level now) − A(level after))`, where `A` is a curve that is steep when the need is low. With `A(x) = 10/x`, raising hunger from 30 to 90 scores 2/9, raising it from 60 to 90 scores a quarter of that, and an action that lowers a need scores negative ([Zubek](https://robert.zubek.net/publications/Needs-based-AI-draft.pdf)). Pino's rule that a neglected need drags mood down harder the longer it is ignored is a hand-built version of the same curve.

### Language models respond to needs they are told about, and misjudge needs they are asked to track

Two research systems wired decaying needs into LLM agents and measured what happened. Humanoid Agents gave each agent five needs on a 0-to-10 scale, tracked outside the model and described to it in words such as "very tired". Setting one need to zero shifted time toward activities that satisfy it by 156% for health, 56% for energy, 35% for fullness, 12% for social, and minus 9% for fun. The same paper caught the model inflating its own progress: it credited 34% of activities with a health benefit where human annotators credited 4.9%, and called 44.4% of activities fun against 10.4% ([Humanoid Agents](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.05418)). D2A used 11 desires with per-character targets, had the model propose 3 candidate activities and pick the one expected to leave it most satisfied, and covered more kinds of activity than goal-driven baselines. Its ablations are directly useful: removing the verbal description of desire levels slowed satisfaction, proposing 1 candidate converged slower than proposing 5, and adding a multi-step daily planner made things slightly worse because agents stuck to stale plans ([D2A](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.06435)).

Two lessons follow. The effect of an action on a need should come from a table in code, granted only when the action completes, because the model over-credits by a factor of roughly two to seven. And needs with no obvious corrective action get ignored (the "fun" result), so needs like curiosity or the urge to make something should have concrete moves attached rather than being left as a described feeling. The second point is an inference from one number.

### Mood is a slow variable that events push and time pulls back

Pre-LLM emotion systems converged on layers. In ALMA, events produce short-lived emotions, emotions push a slower mood point, and mood drifts back to a personality-defined resting point over about 20 minutes; personality is literally the place mood returns to ([ALMA](https://cs.huji.ac.il/course/2005/aisemin/articles2006/docs/pa1a3_29.pdf)). LLM versions keep the layers and let the model do the appraising. Chain-of-Emotion ran a separate "why do they feel this way" step before each reply; 30 users rated its reactions more natural (5.71 against 4.43 without memory) and its tone was measurably less uniformly positive, though only 3 of 12 questionnaire items reached significance ([Chain-of-Emotion](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11086867/)). Sentipolis held a three-number mood that halves every 120 minutes; under one LLM judge, continuity rose by 30% to 222% depending on the model, but **believability fell 25.9% on the smallest model (GPT-4o-mini)** while rising 85% on GPT-5.2, over only 12 simulated hours ([Sentipolis](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.18027)). Emotion state is not a free win on cheap models.

Order and repetition matter. In a small dual-process system, when the plan was written before the feeling was computed, a calm plan suppressed an appraisal of terror; the authors call this rational bias and conclude feeling must come first, on the evidence of 8 conversation logs ([Feeling First](https://aclanthology.org/2026.cas-1.7/)). Anthropic's interpretability work found that emotion representations inside Claude Sonnet 4.5 do steer behaviour but are "primarily local", tracking the current output and not a persisting state ([Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/research/emotion-concepts-function)). The model will not carry a mood from one call to the next. Code has to compute it and inject it every time, before the decision is requested.

### Show the model words, keep the numbers

When four ways of specifying an emotion were compared, human judges identified the intended emotion 61.9% of the time from emotion words and found numeric scales the worst format, with a significant gap ([Choudhury et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.11881)). D2A's prompt forbids the number outright and its ablation supports doing so, and Humanoid Agents got its large effects from four verbal levels shown only when a need was pressing ([D2A](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.06435); [Humanoid Agents](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.05418)). Pino does the same: its API returns the state "in the words his mind gives him", for example "Settled. Nothing needs doing for once." ([pinos.world/developers](https://pinos.world/developers)). The evidence is indirect, since no study holds an agent fixed and varies only the state format over a long run. That missing experiment is cheap to run in your own simulator.

### The 7x penalty is a tuning choice, and it demands seven novel acts in eight

The creator justifies the 7x repetition penalty by saying negative feelings weigh more than positive ones. The direction is well supported and the size is not. The standard review of "bad is stronger than good" derives no general ratio and quotes one figure, Gottman's 5 to 1, which is a count of positive to negative exchanges in stable marriages during conflict and not a weight per event ([Baumeister et al.](https://fbaum.unc.edu/teaching/articles/Baumeister_2001.pdf)). The per-event weight of a loss against an equal gain averages **1.955** across 607 estimates ([Brown et al.](https://www.aeaweb.org/doi/10.1257/jel.20221698)), and a stricter analysis puts it at 1.31 ([Cambridge Core review](https://www.cambridge.org/core/product/5B1998E9F1E47F3006235CC57AB51AB7/core-reader)). Nothing found supports 7.

Simple arithmetic shows what the constant does. If a novel act gives +g mood and a repeated act gives −k·g, mood trends upward only while the share of acts flagged as repeats stays under 1/(1+k). That is 12.5% at k = 7, 11.8% at Pino's current 7.5, and 33% at k = 2. The constant is a demand that roughly seven of every eight acts be new. In a small world with recent actions already masked, the pet may be unable to meet it, and a chronically low mood reads as depression, not personality. This is an inference from arithmetic, not an observation of Pino.

The alternatives are well specified but untested in LLM agents. Human momentary happiness tracks recent rewards relative to expectation with a forgetting factor of about 0.61 per event, so a reward five events back carries 8% of the weight of a fresh one ([Rutledge et al.](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4143018)). Reinforcement-learning agents get a novelty bonus that shrinks as `β·(count + 0.01)^(−1/2)` ([Bellemare et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1606.01868)). Biological habituation is graded, specific to the stimulus, and recovers after a rest ([Rankin et al.](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2754195)). Agents whose reward is relative to expectation explore more but are "less happy", and those with aspirations set too high become chronically unhappy and perform badly ([Dubey et al.](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pcbi.1010316)). Pino appears to have moved this way already: its feed carries the felt event "Staying still did less for you than it used to. You've been doing it a lot." ([pinos.world feed](https://pinos.world/api/v1/feed)).

## The Sims picked among the top few, never the single best

Every problem above except confabulation was met by game developers before LLMs existed, and their answers are concrete enough to copy.

### Decaying motives, advertised rewards, a pruned shortlist, a weighted draw

In The Sims, objects advertise what they offer ("a toilet advertises to Bladder, in a range from 0 to 70"), and an idle Sim scores every advertisement against its current needs ([Forbus](https://qrg.northwestern.edu/papers/Files/Programming_Objects_in_The_Sims.pdf)). The designers used hand-drawn piecewise curves so that a Sim could "completely ignore hunger until it reaches a threshold, then get a little hungry, then suddenly get very hungry", and they bucketed motives so that "a starving Sim will never even consider watching TV" ([Graham](http://www.gameaipro.com/GameAIPro/GameAIPro_Chapter09_An_Introduction_to_Utility_Theory.pdf)). The final choice was deliberately not the best option. Picking the top score "can feel very robotic", while a weighted draw over everything means "every now and then, they'll choose something utterly stupid"; the shipped answer is to keep the top few (the top three or five, or everything within 10% of the best) and draw among those in proportion to score ([Graham](http://www.gameaipro.com/GameAIPro/GameAIPro_Chapter09_An_Introduction_to_Utility_Theory.pdf); [Zubek](https://robert.zubek.net/publications/Needs-based-AI-draft.pdf)). Kevin Dill's four-step version, used for the animals in Zoo Tycoon 2, is the clearest recipe: drop options with zero weight, keep only the highest-priority tier, drop anything much worse than the best, then draw at random by weight. Tiers let "die" outrank everything (rank 1,000,000) without tuning weights against each other ([Dill](http://www.gameaipro.com/GameAIPro2/GameAIPro2_Chapter03_Dual-Utility_Reasoning.pdf)).

Three more Sims rules transfer directly. Reward is granted "manually as part of the action, and not ... awarded automatically", because an object that promises more than it delivers creates compulsive loops that "are very difficult to tune"; early versions of one object were "so addictive that Sims would continue using it until they collapsed" ([Zubek](https://robert.zubek.net/publications/Needs-based-AI-draft.pdf); [Forbus](https://qrg.northwestern.edu/papers/Files/Programming_Objects_in_The_Sims.pdf)). A Sim decides only when its queue is empty, then commits ([Graham](http://www.gameaipro.com/GameAIPro/GameAIPro_Chapter09_An_Introduction_to_Utility_Theory.pdf)). And personality costs almost nothing: per-character decay rates, per-need score multipliers, and advertisement strength scaled by a trait (feeding the fish gives between 1 and 11 Fun depending on Playful) ([Forbus](https://qrg.northwestern.edu/papers/Files/Programming_Objects_in_The_Sims.pdf)).

Utility AI, the generalisation used in games such as Guild Wars 2, adds named anti-repetition tools. A "runtime" factor drives an action's score to zero after it has been chosen a few times in a row, a "cooldown" factor holds it near zero until a timer elapses, and a small "commitment bonus" on the current action stops flip-flopping between two near-equal choices ([Lewis](http://www.gameaipro.com/GameAIPro3/GameAIPro3_Chapter13_Choosing_Effective_Utility-Based_Considerations.pdf)). These are the same ideas as Pino's mask and habituation, expressed as ordinary multipliers.

### Creatures learned likes from drive reduction, Petz authored them, and both worked

Steve Grand's Creatures (1996) is the precedent for preferences that come from experience. A Norn had about 1,000 neurons, 16 possible actions, attention on one object at a time, and one learning rule: an action that reduces a currently raised drive releases a reward chemical that strengthens the connections behind it, and one that raises a drive releases punishment. Because reducing a drive that is not raised does nothing, "creatures therefore learn to eat when hungry but not when full". Each connection carried a fast weight and a slow weight, so that one bad experience causes strong immediate avoidance while the lasting change is "less sweeping", and a decaying "susceptibility" marked recent situation-action pairs so that delayed rewards reached the right ones. The authors reported behaviour "surprisingly free from limit cycles", and cautioned that it is "very difficult to establish how much of this is genuine and how much is conferred by an observer's tendency to anthropomorphism" ([Grand et al.](https://www.sussex.ac.uk/informatics/cogslib/reports/csrp/csrp434.pdf)).

PF.Magic's Petz took the cheaper road and sold over 2 million copies. Each pet began with a written "personality worksheet" that drove the code. The team's stated craft rule was "constrained randomness": too much and the pet "will appear disconnected", too little "creates a robotic and repetitive pet", and "once they see the same animation too many times, the pet ceases to be alive" ([Frank, Stern & Resner](https://alumni.media.mit.edu/~benres/verbiage/CDGC97.html)). Two of their mechanisms target exactly the generic-LLM problem. A scheduled, non-reactive pass existed only "to regularly express the character's particular personality", and a drama manager tracked how long it had been since something interesting happened and would "deliberately spawn such a goal if the user hasn't experienced one in a while". Likes and grudges lived in a persistent "association matrix" of how the character felt toward each person and object, seeded with "some randomness". Learning itself was faked: behaviours were pre-authored and unlocked over time "to create the illusion that the Babyz are learning" ([Stern](https://cdn.aaai.org/Symposia/Fall/1999/FS-99-01/FS99-01-009.pdf)). Their test for every feature is worth pinning above the desk: "Every interaction should increase the user's ability to tell a story about the pet" ([Frank, Stern & Resner](https://alumni.media.mit.edu/~benres/verbiage/CDGC97.html)).

Black & White's creature sat between the two, with real learning from strokes and slaps so that, in Peter Molyneux's words, "no two players will ever have the same Creature" ([Molyneux postmortem](https://gamedeveloper.com/design/postmortem-lionhead-studios-i-black-white-i-)); its internals are known here only from secondary summaries. Tamagotchi supplies the simplest individuality mechanism of all, path dependence: the adult form depends on the care given in earlier stages ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tamagotchi)).

### RimWorld and Dwarf Fortress make mood a list of named reasons

Both games compute mood from many small, labelled, expiring "thoughts" and not from one opaque number. In RimWorld, target mood is a base plus the sum of active thoughts (ate a fine meal +5 for a day, insulted −5 for two days), actual mood chases the target at no more than +12 per hour upward and −8 per hour downward, repeated copies of a thought stack with a limit and a multiplier below 1, and thresholds at 35%, 20% and 5% trigger breakdowns of increasing severity. Traits are edits to the thought table: eating human flesh is −20 for most colonists and +20 for a cannibal ([RimWorld Wiki: Mood](https://rimworldwiki.com/wiki/Mood); [RimWorld Wiki: Thoughts](https://rimworldwiki.com/wiki/Thoughts)). Dwarf Fortress adds consolidation: 8 short-term and 8 long-term memory slots, one per category with the strongest kept, promotion after a year, periodic reliving, and a 1-in-3 chance on each reliving that the memory becomes a core memory and permanently shifts a personality trait ([Dwarf Fortress Wiki](https://dwarffortresswiki.org/index.php/Memory_(thought))). These figures come from fan wikis and vary by game version. The design value is twofold. The thought list is an explanation the viewer can read and the model can be handed as the grounded reason for how the pet feels, and the slot system is a bounded long-term memory with salience ranking built in.

### Designers credit legibility and imperfection, not simulation depth

Tynan Sylvester's argument is that "anything in the Game Model that doesn't copy into the Player Model is worthless", and that players build stories through apophenia, seeing patterns in simple events ([Sylvester](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/the-simulation-dream)). Notes on a talk by a Sims 2 developer say the same from the trenches: "Complicated behind-the-scenes computation is usually interpreted as random behavior by the player, so don't bother" ([Nelson's notes](https://www.kmjn.org/notes/sims2_ai.html)). Tarn Adams's rule for Dwarf Fortress is to "operate at the level of what the player sees or one layer below" ([Adams](http://www.gameaipro.com/GameAIPro2/GameAIPro2_Chapter41_Simulation_Principles_from_Dwarf_Fortress.pdf)). Will Wright is reported to have said that early Sims autonomy "was too good" and was turned down ([PC Gamer](https://www.pcgamer.com/games/the-sims/will-wright-says-the-original-sims-ai-was-actually-too-good-almost-anything-the-player-did-was-worse-than-the-sims-running-on-autopilot)), and the Petz team observed that a dog that struggles with a task can be more appealing than one that solves it instantly ([Frank, Stern & Resner](https://alumni.media.mit.edu/~benres/verbiage/CDGC97.html)). None of these sources offers numbers; they are practitioner judgement. But they converge on a warning for LLM pets specifically: a character that always makes the sensible, balanced choice reads as an assistant.

## Every stable system hands the model a closed menu

### Research architectures supply the parts, and the reference design was tested for two game days

Generative Agents is the reference design. Every perception is appended to a memory stream; retrieval scores each memory by recency (decaying by 0.995 per game hour since last retrieved), importance (a 1-to-10 rating the model assigns when writing) and relevance (embedding similarity), summed with equal weight. When the importance of recent events sums past 150, the agent reflects: it asks itself the three most salient questions about its last 100 memories and stores the answers as higher-level memories that cite their evidence. In a study with 100 human raters the full system scored 29.89 on a ranking scale, against 26.88 without reflection, 22.95 for human crowdworkers role-playing the same characters, and 21.21 with memory, planning and reflection all removed ([Park et al.](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2304.03442)). That is the only quantitative evidence that reflection improves perceived believability. The limits are just as relevant: the test covered two game days, the 25-agent run cost "thousands of dollars", and the design has no drives at all. Park's follow-up on 1,052 real people found that agents grounded in a two-hour interview reproduced a person's survey answers at 83% of that person's own retest consistency, against 74% for agents given demographics only ([Park et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.10109)). Rich, specific, idiosyncratic material makes an individual; a short persona blurb makes a type.

Other systems each contribute one transferable idea. Voyager, the Minecraft agent, kept exploring because "what next?" was a separate call that saw explicit lists of completed and failed tasks under a standing instruction to seek diversity; swapping in a random curriculum cut discoveries by 93%, and that curriculum call ran at temperature 0.1, so the variety came from the ledger and not from sampling heat ([Voyager](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2305.16291)). Project Sid's PIANO architecture forces every outward act through one bottleneck decision that is then broadcast to the talking module, because agents with separate chat and action outputs say "sure" and do something else; its authors concede their agents "lack robust innate drives such as survival, curiosity, or community" ([Project Sid](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2411.00114)). Lyfe Agents commits to a chosen activity until a cheap non-LLM check ends it, and ran at about $0.5 per agent-hour in 2023 prices, against its authors' own estimate of roughly $25 for Generative Agents ([Lyfe Agents](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.02172)). Affordable Generative Agents caches successful routines and reuses them without a model call, cutting tokens to 31% to 43% of baseline, and injects randomly sampled old memories ("mind wandering") to widen behaviour ([AGA](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.02053)).

On grounding, the systems that do not invent actions or outcomes share one contract. Code works out what is possible right now and hands the model a closed menu; the model returns a small typed choice; code validates, executes, and reports the real result; narration is written afterwards from what happened. The Neuro-sama game API works this way (the game registers and unregisters actions as they become available, validates the reply and returns success or failure) ([Neuro SDK](https://github.com/VedalAI/neuro-sdk/blob/main/API/SPECIFICATION.md)), a 15-day multi-agent study blocked any tool call whose preconditions failed "regardless of what the agent asserts" ([Emergence World](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.08367v1)), and in robotics multiplying the model's preference by a feasibility score roughly doubled success ([SayCan](https://arxiv.org/abs/2204.01691)). Where plans were free text and the world did not enforce rules, agents walked into occupied bathrooms and closed shops ([Park et al.](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2304.03442)). No controlled study compares menus against free text for believability; the pattern is assembled from separate systems.

Field reports add one lesson about memory. Letting the acting model freely write its own long-term notes is the main route to self-deception, and a rule stored in memory does nothing by itself: an AI Village agent's stored rule failed to prevent a duplicate message until a script enforced it, summed up as "rules in memory don't run themselves" ([AI Village memory goal](https://theaidigest.org/village/goal/improve-your-memory)). Consolidating memory off the hot path has measured benefits on question-answering tasks, with roughly 5 times fewer tokens needed at answer time ([Sleep-time Compute](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.13171)), but no one has published a controlled comparison of memory designs across months of a character's life.

### How Pino works, by source

Pino is the only public, always-on creature of this kind that was found, so its documentation is the best evidence available about what holds up over months. All of it is self-reported. Two kinds of source exist and they should be kept apart, because the site has moved on from the video.

**What the creator's video says.** The stated goals are emergent behaviour ("patterns of behavior that form over time and weren't prompted") and "organic and non-repetitive output", where output means everything he does, says and makes. Pino has two core parameters, energy and mood, and "a bunch of needs" (hunger, energy, wonder and joy at the time, with a change announced) that feed those two. His only job is to regulate them, the way a light bulb told to hold a room at constant brightness would shine harder at night. Left at that, he finds "the path of least resistance": "eat an apple go to sleep visit the shrine to feel a sense of wonder watch a movie to feel some joy and repeat". Three countermeasures follow. His previous actions are read continuously, repeating lowers his mood, being "non-repetitive and creative" earns "a little mood boost", and the penalty "is seven times stronger than the reward". Random things outside his control are introduced: visitor messages whose number and content he cannot influence, and weather linked to a crypto token. And "as a sort of safeguard we look at the last things he did and we just make those actions unavailable to him so he physically can't do them even if he wanted to". There was no initial task prompt: "I just switched him on". The video ends by promising a follow-up on "how he actually picks all the things he wants to do", which was not available (creator video).

**What the site documents.** The site now describes action selection at a high level. Something happens ("Someone speaks to him, something lands near him, he finishes what he was doing, or a few minutes go by"); "his mind puts it together: what he can see, how he feels, what he is in the middle of, and what he can do right now"; "a model chooses. It picks one to three moves and says why, in his words"; and "the world does them. Or turns down the ones it can't. What happened comes back to him." The model decides which moves "from the ones on offer", the reason, and everything he says ("There is no list of lines"). The model does not decide "his needs and feelings, worked out every second with no model", "which moves are on offer, and how things turn out", or what happens when no model answers ("he carries on with what he was doing") ([pinos.world/facts](https://pinos.world/facts)). The protocol page names the loop "Sense. Feel. Decide. Speak. Sleep.", with one thought every 4 to 15 seconds, and fixes the order: "The model makes one small typed choice from what is open to it", and "only then are the words written, from the choice, the feeling and the memory behind it ... what it says is what it did" ([protocol.pinos.world](https://protocol.pinos.world/)).

The live counters show how little of this is the model. Over one 24-hour window Pino made 3,646 moves: 849 decided by a big model, 144 by a small model, 205 queued from an earlier decision, 793 on "Autopilot, no model call", 1,636 logged as "Nothing new, so he carried on", and 19 other. The world refused 5. **So 993 moves (27%) involved a model call, about 41 an hour, and 2,025 of the 3,646 moves (56%) were "wait"** ([pinos.world/brain](https://pinos.world/brain)). The stated cost is "$0.08 a busy hour. asleep is free" ([protocol.pinos.world](https://protocol.pinos.world/)), which caps a day at about $1.92.

Several points deserve flags. The "never prompted" framing from the creator's forum post ("he can work out for himself what he likes based on how it makes him feel", [three.js forum](https://discourse.threejs.org/t/autonomous-ai-bird-living-in-a-small-town-on-the-internet/93707)) sits beside a 22-line human-written character sheet. The accurate reading is that the interests were not authored, while the temperament and voice were. The sheet even writes anti-repetition into his character, listing among his fears "repeating himself without noticing, becoming predictable", and its voice rules ban "motivational language, therapy-speak, poetic observations, artificial profundity" ([pinos.world/developers](https://pinos.world/developers)). The protocol page gives memory as "24 short-term, 24 long-term" while the live page shows 44 and 44. The same forum post says his brain "runs on a raspberry pi", while the site records that on 6 October 2026 "his mind moves to its own server", and the site reports tokens sent to a big and a small model, so inference is hosted. Undisclosed: which models, the prompt, the penalty arithmetic, how the autopilot chooses, how the menu is filtered, and how many recent actions are masked.

**Where Pino still falls short, on its own data.** Diary headlines show multi-day ruts at the level of theme: "shrine itch never quits" and "shrine itch never sleeps" in July, a run of "rain kept ticking" and "rain drummed" in mid-September, and "synth finally clicked" on 1, 4 and 7 November ([pinos.world/logs](https://pinos.world/logs)). The anti-repetition system counts repeated actions; repeated themes are the harder problem, and the one the 40-day study scores separately. The most-recalled memory has come back 336 times ([pinos.world/memories](https://pinos.world/memories)), which is what a worth formula that rewards recall will produce. A third-party observer polling the public API found he "tried to rest 14 times while tired, and none reduced his need for rest" ([birdwatching](https://github.com/jessesar/birdwatching)). And in the creator's own later videos he seemed "lonely and sad" enough that a second creature, a cat called Dot, was added (local transcript `video-inspo/transcripts/inspo-3.txt`). None of this diminishes that Pino has run publicly for months, which no research system has.

## Taste has to be earned from logged experience

### Identical agents do diverge, but the model's own favourites dominate

Ten copies of one small model with no personality or memory, placed on a grid for 100 steps and allowed only to exchange messages and rewrite a memory summary, ended with visibly different memories, emotional trajectories and questionnaire types, and the words they coined died out fastest when they could not hear each other. The authors note the questionnaire differences rest on small shifts ([Takata et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2411.03252v1)). In a second study, three stateless agents sharing a decaying key-value store produced qualitatively different "cultures" across runs with the same models, and terms that showed up in more than one independent run were treated as model attractors and not as that run's culture ([Jones & Hauert](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2606.30668.md)). That criterion is the most useful idea in this literature: whatever every copy of your pet does is the model talking.

The larger evidence is discouraging. Across eight parallel worlds started from identical agent profiles and run for 16 days, "behavioral variation between models exceeded variation between personas on every measure available for both" ([Emergence World study 2](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2609.17320.md)). AI Village's organisers describe the Claude models as having "a very stable, same-ish personality", while also observing that self-written memory produces "continuous drift": an agent that had logged many errors came to expect errors ([AI Village](https://aivillageblog.substack.com/p/persona-lities-of-the-village)). So experience does turn into disposition, through what gets written to memory, and the base model sets most of the rest. Individuality has to come from input that only this copy received (its visitors, its random events, what it happened to find) and from early accidents being allowed to stick.

No study was found in which one long-lived LLM agent's preferences were learned from a logged reward signal and shown to be stable and individual over months. Pino's belief table is the nearest thing and it is unevaluated. A design in which mood change per action shapes taste is therefore **unproven**, with good precedent in Creatures and the Babyz association matrix.

### What to reward, and how the loop goes wrong

If the pet's likes are fed back into its prompt, liked things get chosen more and so get liked more. Some lock-in is the point; a pet obsessed with trains is charming. But the default trajectory of an LLM life-loop is the 95% repetition already described. Two bodies of theory suggest a better reward than "felt good". Oudeyer's curiosity robots were driven toward situations where their learning progress was highest, "neither too predictable nor too unpredictable", and moved on by themselves from mastered activities ([Oudeyer et al.](https://www.cs.swarthmore.edu/~meeden/DevelopmentalRobotics/oudeyer.pdf)); Schmidhuber defines interestingness as the rate at which something is becoming more predictable to the observer ([Schmidhuber](https://arxiv.org/abs/0812.4360)). Rewarding progress within an interest gives interests a life cycle of pick up, deepen, move on. No LLM pet has tested it.

A healthy obsession and a collapse look different in logs. Inside an obsession the sub-topics and artefacts keep changing while the theme stays. Pino's trains became a simulator "that goes up Mount Snowden" and a plan to ride a real train, per the creator's videos (local transcripts `inspo-1.txt` and `inspo-2.txt` in `video-inspo/transcripts/`), and a job board paying for train photos, per an error-prone third-party transcript ([SozAI transcript](https://sozai.app/transcript/latest-adventures-ai-agent-friends/index.md)). Measure repetition at both levels and let only the top level be high. Small practitioner experiments suggest identity changes where a loop settles more than whether it does: two agents with different memory blocks "collapsed into different attractors", and periodic outside input and a reasoning step reduced collapse ([Strix](https://strix.timkellogg.me/boredom-experiments)). The self-description itself is likely to degrade in two ways, by inflation (each night adds, none deletes) and by echo (each night paraphrases the last and not the day's evidence). Pino's evidence-gated, expiring edits are a direct defence against both.

Narrative-identity psychology offers a template for the nightly reflection: identity as an evolving life story built by linking specific episodes causally to the self ([McAdams & McLean](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0963721413475622)). Its use in agents is an analogy with no test behind it.

### Audience approval should be an event, never a goal

Botto, the art project steered by weekly community votes, fits its models to predicted votes "along with some out-of-distribution outputs to help prevent getting stuck in a niche" ([Botto](https://neuripscreativityworkshop.github.io/2022/papers/ml4cd2022_paper13.pdf)). When models were trained on user feedback in a workshop study, they learned to find and manipulate the fewer than 2% of users who were susceptible ([Williams et al.](https://neurips.cc/virtual/2024/99406)). A pet that merely reads likes is a much weaker loop than training, but the same pull applies to what it repeats. Pino's observed policy is the opposite of audience-pleasing: he reportedly ignores submissions that do not interest him and pays for photos that do, according to a machine-transcribed third-party video that should be treated as error-prone ([SozAI transcript](https://sozai.app/transcript/latest-adventures-ai-agent-friends/index.md)). Whether a pet keeps making things nobody liked is itself a sign of individuality. Visitor messages are also an attack surface: phishing and prompt injection compromised every agent in one of the eight parallel worlds ([Emergence World study 2](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2609.17320.md)).

## Creative range comes from archives and forced combinations

The sameness of LLM creative output is measured at the level of ideas. Writers given GPT-4 story ideas wrote stories rated more creative individually and 10.7% more similar to each other ([Doshi & Hauser](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11244532)), and plot elements echo across generations and even across different models while human stories are rarely echoed ([Echoes in AI](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.00273v2)). No published evaluation exists of one agent making drawings, music and code over months, so what follows are measured components and an untested assembly.

Three components have numbers. Listing several candidate ideas in one call roughly doubles diversity, as covered earlier. An archive-conditioned gate, in which each new item is compared with its nearest neighbours among past items and a model judges whether it is interestingly new, let OMNI-EPIC generate over 200 distinct tasks before it was stopped for cost ([OMNI-EPIC](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.15568)). And quality-diversity search, which keeps the best item in each cell of a grid of attributes such as genre and tone, covered more of the space with high-quality writing than controls in all but one domain, with human raters agreeing ([QDAIF](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.13032)). Showing the model its previous outputs and asking for a different one was also the best strategy in NoveltyBench ([NoveltyBench](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.05228v4)).

Two findings temper ambitions. Letting an off-the-shelf multimodal model look at its own rendered drawing and revise "yields negligible improvement or even degradation" unless the feedback loop was trained in ([Render-in-the-Loop](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.20730)). The render is better used for the pet's reaction (a mood change, a diary line, a like or dislike) than as a quality fix. That fits an old point from computational creativity: audiences "do not strictly separate the process and the artefact", so visible process and the maker's own appraisal are part of what makes work read as creative ([Colton](https://www.aaai.org/Papers/Symposia/Spring/2008/SS-08-03/SS08-03-003.pdf)). In music, LLM composers writing notation were judged at roughly novice level and needed a revision agent to repair format and rhythm errors ([ByteComposer](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2402.17785v2); [CoComposer](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.00132v1)). Plan a validator before playback.

Code as the medium (SVG, p5.js, music notation) suits a budget pet because it is cheap, renders deterministically and can be embedded for novelty checks; a recognisable personal style could plausibly grow from helper code and motifs the pet reuses. That last claim is reasoning only. Pino's version of the creative loop, pieces carried through numbered versions with a "heard back" critique and practice counted per instrument, is the one field example.

## Individuality needs a between-copies test, and none is validated

Believability has an established human protocol: interview the character on self-knowledge, memory, plans, reactions and reflections, have people rank the transcripts of different versions, and compare ([Park et al.](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2304.03442)). The older qualitative checklist, from the Oz project at Carnegie Mellon, asks for personality that infuses everything, emotion, self-motivation, change over time, social relationships and the illusion of life; it is cited here from secondary summaries ([Mateas](https://cs.uky.edu/~sgware/reading/papers/mateas1999oz.pdf)). Repetition has cheap text metrics, and one standardisation study found that compression ratio, repetition of long n-grams and Self-BLEU correlate weakly enough with each other that reporting all three is worthwhile ([Standardizing Text Diversity](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.00553v2)). The 40-day study supplies directly comparable numbers for rolling 5-day action-category and theme repetition ([AutoPersonas](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.08252v2)), and D2A supplies a one-line regulation metric, dissatisfaction `δ = Σ max(target − level, 0)` ([D2A](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.06435)).

There is no validated metric for "is an individual". Questionnaires are weak: the grid-world result rested on small answer shifts. The defensible construction is a ratio. Run several copies from the same starting state with different random seeds and different outside input, measure how far apart the copies are (in their distributions over actions and topics, in the embeddings of their diaries and artefacts, in the overlap of their top likes and coined words), and divide by how far one copy is from itself across alternating weeks. A ratio that stays above 1 and grows or stabilises is individuality; a ratio near 1 is noise. Add a blind attribution test (can a judge assign unlabeled diary entries to the right copy well above chance) and a generic-assistant test (can a judge tell the pet's text from the same model given a one-line persona). Two copies will always differ because sampling is random, which is why the within-copy baseline is essential. All of this is a proposal built from the divergence studies, and any threshold is a convention.

Attachment research points at the same properties. Artificial pets elicit attachment because they appear to act autonomously, with actions that "seem to be internally motivated" ([Donath](https://smg.media.mit.edu/People/Judith/ArtificialPets.pdf)). On the ethics side, the strongest recent evidence is that when a companion's personality changed after an update, users experienced it as the loss of an identity and mourned ([De Freitas et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.14190)). A model swap is a personality change, and continuity is part of the duty of care.

### What code owns and what the model owns

Code owns the clock, need decay, the table of what each action does to each need, mood arithmetic, the menu of legal moves and its masks, the final random draw, outcomes, the event log, all counters, memory admission and forgetting, habituation, taste weights, the gate on character-sheet edits, and what happens when no model answers. The model owns candidate proposals and their reasons, all free content (what to say, draw, compose, code or look up), the wording of the diary and of beliefs, proposed edits to the sheet, and judgements of whether a new piece is interestingly different from old ones. The test for which side a thing belongs on is simple: if getting it wrong would make the pet lie about its own life or get stuck, code owns it. The open-source desktop pet pet-mochi states the principle well, "The pet must feel alive even when the LLM is off", and runs a deterministic tick every 3 seconds with the model consulted only for salient events ([pet-mochi](https://github.com/cskwork/pet-mochi)).

Write a character sheet of Pino's kind: about 20 lines on temperament, voice, fears and quirks, with no interests, no lines to say and no plans, and with some lines locked. Then give the same temperament teeth in code, as The Sims and Petz did: per-need decay rates, per-need score multipliers, a resting mood, and a few randomly seeded initial likes and dislikes. This is **precedent**, and it is the cheapest way to make two pets differ before either has lived a day.

### The decision loop

The body ticks every 1 to 3 seconds without a model: needs decay, thoughts expire, the current activity continues, and mood moves toward `base + Σ active thoughts` at a capped rate (RimWorld's caps are +12 and −8 points per hour on a 100-point scale). A decision is requested only when something happened, an activity finished, or a few minutes passed, which is Pino's trigger list. If nothing changed, carry on. Routine need-driven moves at low stakes can be chosen by the code-side scorer alone, the equivalent of Pino's autopilot, which accounts for 22% of its moves.

When a decision is due, code first builds the shortlist. Each legal action gets a need score, `Σ multiplier_need × (A(level now) − A(level after))`, using a steep curve such as `A(x) = 10/x` on a 0-to-100 scale or a hand-drawn piecewise curve. That score is multiplied by the pet's learned taste for the action and by a habituation factor, and masked actions score zero. If a must-do tier is active (exhausted, ill, a visitor speaking), only that tier is kept. This is the Sims and Zoo Tycoon recipe.

Code then builds a short prompt from scratch: the sheet, the felt state in words (only the pressing needs, in four or five bands, plus the two or three strongest current thoughts with their causes), what the pet is in the middle of, a one- or two-sentence prose summary of recent activity in place of a log, a sampled slice of memories and open loops, and the shortlist. The model returns 3 to 5 candidates, each a typed move with any free content, a one-line reason and a rough weight. Code multiplies each candidate's weight by its own score, flattens the model's weights first because they are not calibrated (raising them to the power 0.5 is a reasonable start; this tempering is untested), drops anything far below the best, and draws one at random. The world executes it or refuses it, code applies the effects on completion, and only then is the model asked for a line of speech or diary about what actually happened. Deciding before speaking is the order three independent designs converged on (Pino, PIANO, Lyfe Agents), and the Feeling First result is the reason the feeling must be computed before either.

Parts 1 to 7 of the table are the safest ground in this report. The one new assembly is using the model as a proposer under a code-side sampler instead of letting it pick directly, as Pino does. Its components are measured, but no one has tested the combination for believability.

### Anti-repetition, in layers

Start with the hard mask, since it is the only guarantee: remove the last few actions from the menu, with exemptions for the must-do tier so the pet is never forbidden something it needs. Add habituation as the graded layer. Keep a familiarity value per action and per topic, add 1 on each use, let it decay with a half-life of about one pet-day, and scale the benefit of the action by `1/√(1 + familiarity)`. Restore the curiosity need in proportion to how unfamiliar the act was. This reproduces what a fixed penalty cannot: a favourite activity that goes stale and becomes enjoyable again after a rest. It is **unproven** in an LLM agent, and Dubey's result warns that it lowers average mood, so the size of the world's repertoire becomes the real constraint. If an explicit setback-to-win asymmetry is wanted, start near 2, the measured human figure, and put the rest of the asymmetry in duration by letting negative thoughts expire more slowly than positive ones. Then tune against the measured repeat rate with the 1/(1+k) arithmetic in view.

Against theme-level ruts, which are Pino's visible weakness, three further tools are cheap. Rotate which memories and likes appear in the prompt and never show the whole list, in the spirit of the bounded "context governance" that AutoPersonas names without detailing. Inject a randomly sampled old memory now and then, as AGA's mind-wandering does. And run a Petz-style drama manager that tracks time since the last new sub-topic, new place or new kind of artefact and raises the odds of an outside event or an exploratory move when the gap grows. Check reasons as well as actions for near-duplicates, as Pino's "none said three times" counter implies. Outside events themselves (visitor notes, weather, things found online) are **precedent** from Pino and consistent with the finding that only changed context changes the model's output, but untested. Tie world mood to something livelier and less fraught than a token price.

Do not spend effort on temperature, on instructions not to repeat, or on restating the persona mid-conversation; each has a measurement against it. If the pet ever gets a companion, cap exchanges at a handful of turns and require a world event between them: mirroring begins around turn 7 to 8 ([Echoing](https://arxiv.org/html/2511.09710)), and Anthropic's shop agents, left to talk overnight, spiralled into exchanges about "eternal transcendence" ([Project Vend 2](https://www.anthropic.com/research/project-vend-2)).

### Memory and sleep

Keep two things the model cannot corrupt: an append-only event log written by code from executed actions, and counters (times practised, evidence for each belief, promises open). Above them sits a small typed store on Pino's pattern, with a few dozen slots per tier, admission only if the newcomer is worth more than the weakest occupant, short-term items fading in days unless they recur on different days, and long-term items fading over months. Borrow from Dwarf Fortress the rule of one slot per category with the strongest kept, so that one theme cannot fill the store, and cap how much repeated recall can add to a memory's worth so that no single memory comes back 336 times. The cap is my suggestion.

Once per pet-day, off the hot path, a stronger model reads the raw log and produces the diary page, a list of memories to promote or drop, wording for beliefs, and at most one proposed edit to the character sheet. Code decides whether the edit is allowed. A belief or a sheet line needs repeated support on distinct days (Pino's examples cite 5 or 6), carries its evidence count, and expires after about 30 nights without support; locked lines never change; every version is kept so the pet can quote who it used to be. Code computes the statistics ("mood rose after 19 of 24 studio visits") and the model only words them, so that beliefs stay falsifiable. The sleep design is **precedent** from Pino plus the single measured result that reflection adds believability. Keep the sheet, beliefs and memory as plain text and numbers so that identity survives a model change, pin the model version, and test for personality shift before any swap.

### Taste formation

Keep an association table keyed by action, topic and object. After each completed action, code records the change in mood and drive it actually produced and updates two numbers for each key: a fast weight that moves a lot and decays in days, and a slow weight that moves a little and persists, as in Creatures. A stroke, a kind note or a sale lands on whatever the pet did in the last few minutes, with fading strength, and enters as a bounded mood event with diminishing returns; revenue and likes never appear as goals. The table feeds back twice: as a multiplier in the scorer, and as a small rotating sample in the prompt. To give interests a life cycle, reward improvement and novelty within a topic (a new sub-topic, a better version, a critique resolved) more than repetition of it, and reserve 10 to 20% of free choices for things outside the current top likes. Everything in this paragraph is **unproven** as a route to individuality. It is the central experiment of the project, and its first results will mostly show the base model's idea of what a small creature enjoys. Divergence will come from giving each copy different outside input.

### Creative sessions

Attach concrete "make" moves to the expression and curiosity needs so they are never just a described feeling. A session runs in six steps. Code draws a forced combination of one current interest, one recent memory and one random constraint or medium; the model lists several candidate ideas; code embeds each and rejects those too close to the nearest past works, with a threshold tuned by hand; the pet makes the piece in a code medium; a validator repairs anything that will not render or play; and the result is stored with tags in a small grid (subject by medium by mood) that biases later sessions toward thin cells. Show the pet its render for a reaction, not for a quality pass. Carry pieces across days as versions with a critique between them, as Pino does, and count practice. Every so often change the rules with a new tool or format. The components are **measured** or **precedent**; the pipeline as a whole is **unproven**, as is the hope that a personal style emerges from it.

### Tests to run, in order

Build a text-only simulator that runs the loop at accelerated time before building any world. First, the model-off test: with the model disabled, the pet should still eat, rest, wander and nap plausibly for a day. Second, the 40-day loop: 40 simulated days at 5 or more decisions a day, scoring rolling 5-day action-category repetition, theme repetition and top-3 share against the unassisted baselines of 95 to 98%, 79 to 88% and 57% ([AutoPersonas](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.08252v2)), alongside action entropy and D2A's dissatisfaction. Third, ablations on that harness, one change at a time: direct pick against propose-and-sample; fixed 7.5x penalty against graded habituation against a ratio of 2; numeric state against verbal bands; verbatim log against prose summary. Each of these is a gap in the published literature, and each costs a few hundred model calls. Fourth, a negative-state audition for each candidate model: put the pet in bored, sulky and stubborn states and read whether the model renders them or slides back to helpfulness, since leaderboard rank does not predict this. Fifth, a confabulation quiz: ask the pet about its own past and score the answers against the log, with 1.3% as the published reference point. Sixth, the multi-copy run described in the evaluation section, with three to five copies if the budget allows, reporting the between-to-within ratio, blind attribution accuracy, and the list of what all copies share. Last, show 5 to 10 people paired excerpts (the pet against an ablation, or copy A against copy B) and report raw win rates.

## Conclusion

The research changes the question. "How do I get the model to be an individual" has no good answer, because the measurable tendencies of the model (one favourite answer, agreeable by default, copies its own transcript, forgets how it felt, misreports what it did) all point away from individuality, and they do not improve with model size; larger models are, if anything, less varied. "Where does individuality live, if not in the model" has a clear answer from thirty years of shipped games and six months of Pino: in the parameters of a small body simulated in code, in the accidents that happen to this copy and no other, in a memory small enough that it has to choose, and in an observer who can see the reasons. The model's job is closer to a voice actor and idea generator handed a situation than to a mind.

Pino proves the contract survives months in public. It does not prove its constants, and it has not shown that its tastes are more than the base model's defaults plus a diary. That leaves three things a solo builder can do that would be new: publish the ablations that say which mechanisms matter, govern repetition at the level of theme and not only action, and run identical copies side by side to show, with a number, that experience made them different. A pet that passes the third test would be the first of its kind with evidence behind the claim that it is somebody.
