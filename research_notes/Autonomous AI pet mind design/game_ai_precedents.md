## 1. The Sims (1 through 4): motive decay, smart-object advertisements, scoring, final choice, and why pure optimisation looked robotic

### Takeaway
The Sims pattern is: code-run decaying motives, objects that advertise per-motive rewards, a non-linear "urgency" curve that makes low motives count far more than high ones, distance attenuation per advertisement, and a final pick that is a weighted-random draw from the few best options rather than the single best. Developers who worked on the series say explicitly that picking the absolute best "can feel very robotic", and Will Wright is reported to have said early autonomy was so good that the player had nothing to do, so it was tuned down.

### Cited Findings

**Architecture and loop**
- [P-read] The top-level loop is three steps: while there are actions in the queue, pop and perform one "and maybe get a reward"; if the queue is empty, run action selection on current needs; if still nothing, do fallback actions. Action selection itself is: examine nearby objects and collect what they advertise, score each advertisement against current needs, pick one, push its action sequence on the queue. — [Zubek, "Needs-Based AI" (draft of Game Programming Gems 8 chapter)](https://robert.zubek.net/publications/Needs-based-AI-draft.pdf)
- [P-read] Zubek (ex-EA/Maxis) credits The Sims with the "very useful innovation on knowledge representation, where behaviors and their advertisements are literally distributed 'in the world' in the game, and therefore very easily configurable." — [Zubek](https://robert.zubek.net/publications/Needs-based-AI-draft.pdf)
- [P-read] In The Sims 1 every object carries local data plus behaviours, each behaviour consisting of "a procedure that implements it ... a procedure that checks to see whether or not it is possible, and a set of advertisements that describe its properties in terms of what need(s) of a Sim it will satisfy. Sims (not under direct player control) choose what to do by selecting, from all of the possible behaviors in all of the objects, the behavior that maximizes their current happiness." The chosen procedure then runs in the Sim's own thread. — [Forbus, "Some notes on programming objects in The Sims" (Northwestern, 2001)](https://qrg.northwestern.edu/papers/Files/Programming_Objects_in_The_Sims.pdf)
- [P-read] The Sims 1 need list as exposed to object authors: "energy, comfort, hunger, hygiene, bladder, room, social, fun, mood". — [Forbus](https://qrg.northwestern.edu/papers/Files/Programming_Objects_in_The_Sims.pdf)
- [P-sum] Forbus and Wright's lecture outline lists the AI design requirements as "Robust (graceful failure)", "User defined environments", "Understandable (to the player)", "Expandable (code not just data)", and classifies interactions as Normal ("90% of the Interactions"), Advertised (autonomous), Manual (player selected), Pushed (high priority, immediate, e.g. carpool, fire) and Functional (goal oriented, outside needs, e.g. clean, repair, cook). — [Forbus and Wright, "Under the hood of The Sims" outline, CS 395, 2002](https://users.cs.northwestern.edu/~forbus/c95-gd/lectures/The_Sims_Under_the_Hood_files/outline.htm)

**Motive decay**
- [P-read] Needs are "an array of numeric values, which decay over time", conventionally in [0, 100] with "lower is worse and more urgent"; performing an action refills the need. — [Zubek](https://robert.zubek.net/publications/Needs-based-AI-draft.pdf)
- [P-read] Per-agent decay rates are the cheapest personality knob: "if an agent's hunger doesn't decay as quickly, they will not need to eat as often, and will have more time for other pursuits. We can use this to model a bare-bones personality profile ... It can also be used for difficulty tuning: agents whose needs decay more quickly are harder to please." — [Zubek](https://robert.zubek.net/publications/Needs-based-AI-draft.pdf)

**How advertisements are authored (The Sims 1)**
- [P-read] Each interaction has an Attenuation setting that "sets how quickly the advertisement fades. A setting of none makes the strength of the advertisement independent of distance", with presets high, moderate, low, or a custom value. — [Forbus](https://qrg.northwestern.edu/papers/Files/Programming_Objects_in_The_Sims.pdf)
- [P-read] Advertised values are per motive with Min and Max, optionally scaled by a personality trait: "a toilet advertises to Bladder, in a range from 0 to 70 ... If there is no variation by personality, the Max is used. If it does vary by personality, then the motive is scaled linearly according to the number of points for that aspect of the personality. For instance, the aquarium's Feed Fish interaction provides between 1 and 11 on Fun, depending on the Playful personality component." Traits come as polar opposites (nice/grouchy, active/lazy) so the sign can flip. — [Forbus](https://qrg.northwestern.edu/papers/Files/Programming_Objects_in_The_Sims.pdf)
- [P-read] Objects control what they advertise based on their own state: a fridge advertises "prepare food", later also "clean me", and when broken only "fix me". Advertisements can also be filtered per agent (e.g. stoves do not advertise "cook" to children) via simple attribute masks. — [Zubek](https://robert.zubek.net/publications/Needs-based-AI-draft.pdf)

**Scoring formulas**
- [P-read] Trivial scoring (sum of future need values) is rejected because it "doesn't differentiate between urgent and non-urgent needs". — [Zubek](https://robert.zubek.net/publications/Needs-based-AI-draft.pdf)
- [P-read] Attenuated scoring: `score = Σ_needs A_need(future_value)`, where A is "non-linear and non-increasing: starts out high when the need level is low, then drops quickly". Example `A(x) = 10/x`. — [Zubek](https://robert.zubek.net/publications/Needs-based-AI-draft.pdf)
- [P-read] Recommended form, attenuated need-delta scoring: `score = Σ_needs ( A_need(current_value) − A_need(future_value) )`. With A(x)=10/x, raising hunger 30→90 scores 1/3 − 1/9 = 2/9, raising it 60→90 scores 1/18 (a quarter as much), and an action that lowers hunger 100→90 scores negative "so it will not be selected unless there is nothing else to do." — [Zubek](https://robert.zubek.net/publications/Needs-based-AI-draft.pdf)
- [P-read] "These attenuation functions are a major tuning knob in needs-based AI", but analytic curves are hard for designers; the recommended practice is piecewise-linear curves stored in a spreadsheet. — [Zubek](https://robert.zubek.net/publications/Needs-based-AI-draft.pdf)
- [P-read] The Sims uses hand-tuned piecewise-linear curves for exactly this reason: designers "want the ability to have a Sim completely ignore hunger until it reaches a threshold, then get a little hungry, then suddenly get very hungry. There's no way to build and tune that specific curve with a simple mathematical formula ... [a piecewise linear curve] allows you to create any shaped curve you want and is exactly what The Sims uses." A smooth curve leaves a small chance of eating when nearly full, which "would eventually get chosen". — [Graham, "An Introduction to Utility Theory", Game AI Pro ch. 9](http://www.gameaipro.com/GameAIPro/GameAIPro_Chapter09_An_Introduction_to_Utility_Theory.pdf)
- [P-read] Distance attenuation: `score = D( Σ_needs(...) )` with the "physically-inspired" example `D(x) = x / distance²`. Warning: it "can be difficult to tune" and may produce "bird in hand" behaviour "where the agent always prefers a much worse action nearby rather than a better one further away." — [Zubek](https://robert.zubek.net/publications/Needs-based-AI-draft.pdf)
- [P-read] Per-agent personality multiplier on scores: `new_score(agent, need) = old_score(agent, need) × tuning(agent, need)`. — [Zubek](https://robert.zubek.net/publications/Needs-based-AI-draft.pdf)
- [snippet] A leaked Maxis technical design document describes happiness as roughly the average of a person's motives, with "Find Best Action" using "a 'cutoff point' for each motive above which additional motive value contributes nothing", and an interaction's score coming from comparing happiness before and after applying its motive advertisements. The PDF returned 404 when opened, so this is unverified. — [The Sims Tech Design, "Happy" chapter (linkedbyair.net mirror)](https://linkedbyair.net/bin/The%20Sims%20Design%20Documents/117522772-The-Sims-Tech-Design-ChAux2-Happy.pdf)

**Final selection: not the single best**
- [P-read] Standard arbitration options: "Winner-takes-all: the highest-scoring action always gets picked" or "Weighted-random: do a random selection from the top n (eg. top 3) high-scoring advertisements, with probability proportional to score." Verdict: "weighted-random is a good compromise between having some predictability about what will happen, and not having the agent look unpleasantly deterministic." — [Zubek](https://robert.zubek.net/publications/Needs-based-AI-draft.pdf)
- [P-read] "For some games (like The Sims), choosing the absolute best action can feel very robotic due to the likelihood that the action will always be selected in that situation." Pure weighted random over all options has "the opposite problem ... every now and then, they'll choose something utterly stupid." The fix: "taking a subset of the highest scoring actions and choosing one of those with a weighted random. This can either be a tuned value, such as choosing from among the top five scoring actions, or it can be percentile based where you take the highest score and also consider things that scored within, say, 10% of it." — [Graham, Game AI Pro ch. 9](http://www.gameaipro.com/GameAIPro/GameAIPro_Chapter09_An_Introduction_to_Utility_Theory.pdf)
- [P-read] Motive bucketing in The Sims: "motives are bucketed based on their utility value. The highest scoring motives are grouped into a bucket, and none of the motives below are considered ... a starving Sim will never even consider watching TV unless they fail to find anything that can solve their hunger." Worked example: Hunger bucket scores 0.8, Fun 0.4; the Sim picks only among Hunger actions even if a Fun action scores higher, and falls through to the next bucket only if no Hunger action is valid. "The buckets themselves are scored based on a response curve created by designers." — [Graham, Game AI Pro ch. 9](http://www.gameaipro.com/GameAIPro/GameAIPro_Chapter09_An_Introduction_to_Utility_Theory.pdf)
- [P-read] Commitment in The Sims Medieval: "a Sim would only attempt to make a decision when their interaction queue was empty. Once they chose an action, they would commit to performing that action. Once the Sim completed (or failed to complete) their action, they would choose a new action." — [Graham, Game AI Pro ch. 9](http://www.gameaipro.com/GameAIPro/GameAIPro_Chapter09_An_Introduction_to_Utility_Theory.pdf)

**Reward on completion, false advertising, addiction loops**
- [P-read] "It's important that the actual reward be granted manually as part of the action, and not be awarded automatically": interrupted actions are not rewarded, and objects can falsely advertise. False advertising produces "very intriguing 'addictive' behaviors" because the unrewarded agent picks the same action "again, and then again", but "False advertisements create action loops that are very difficult to tune." — [Zubek](https://robert.zubek.net/publications/Needs-based-AI-draft.pdf)
- [P-read] "Creating addictive objects is relatively easy – give some object's behavior high advertisements along some dimension, and you'll see that behavior chosen over and over again ... What is more difficult is to create collections of objects that are balanced, where Sims interact with them in natural-looking patterns (or at least more amusingly varied than an obsessive-compulsive exhibits)." Early versions of the example Joy Booth "were so addictive that Sims would continue using it until they collapsed." — [Forbus](https://qrg.northwestern.edu/papers/Files/Programming_Objects_in_The_Sims.pdf)
- [P-read] Proposed but unimplemented in Sims 1, "Skeptical Sims": "currently Sims have no way of knowing whether or not the experience was worth it"; the suggested exercise is to record motive values before and after a behaviour in a Relationship object and "use this information to scale the advertisements when evaluating future interactions". — [Forbus](https://qrg.northwestern.edu/papers/Files/Programming_Objects_in_The_Sims.pdf)

**Action chaining and failure**
- [P-read] Multi-step actions (take food, prepare, cook, eat) should be chained, not atomic, and steps "can be interrupted, or they can fail". Lazy chaining, where each step creates the next at run time, "makes interesting failures easier. For example, the stove can look up some internal variable (eg. repair level) to determine failure, and randomly push a 'create a kitchen fire' action instead." Interrupted progress is stored in the world as objects ("prepped food" sits on the counter until picked up later). — [Zubek](https://robert.zubek.net/publications/Needs-based-AI-draft.pdf)

**Legibility**
- [P-read] "the player's experience with this AI really benefits from adding some feedback to the agents ... the player will have no such access, and is likely to build an entirely different mental model of what the agent is doing. Little bits of feedback, like thought bubbles about what needs is being fulfilled, are easy to implement, and go a long way". — [Zubek](https://robert.zubek.net/publications/Needs-based-AI-draft.pdf)
- [P-read] Needs-based AI is poor at triggered or scripted behaviour; "it would be better to just manually manufacture appropriate action sequences, and forcibly push them on the agent's action queue." — [Zubek](https://robert.zubek.net/publications/Needs-based-AI-draft.pdf)

**Designer commentary on autonomy and optimisation**
- [S] In a New York Times interview for the game's 25th anniversary (2025), as reported by PC Gamer, Will Wright said "In early versions of the game, the autonomy was too good" and "Almost anything the player did was worse than the Sims running on autopilot." The NYT original could not be opened, so wording is taken from secondary coverage. — [PC Gamer](https://www.pcgamer.com/games/the-sims/will-wright-says-the-original-sims-ai-was-actually-too-good-almost-anything-the-player-did-was-worse-than-the-sims-running-on-autopilot); [GamesRadar](https://gamesradar.com/games/the-sims/the-sims-design-mastermind-will-wright-says-the-first-sims-ever-made-were-too-good-at-staying-alive-and-needed-to-be-stupider-for-the-game-to-be-fun)
- [S] Notes on a talk by Matt Brown (EA/Maxis, The Sims 2): "Perception is key: how to seem intelligent, not how to be intelligent"; "Consistency leads to player storytelling and ascription of personality"; "Complicated behind-the-scenes computation is usually interpreted as random behavior by the player, so don't bother"; a detailed model for which urinal a Sim chooses "wasn't reliably getting the responses they wanted", so the team "replaced it with a random-number generator"; "Players have short memories and quickly move on, so your AI should too"; some randomness "helps avoid brittleness". — [Mark J. Nelson's notes on Matt Brown's talk](https://www.kmjn.org/notes/sims2_ai.html)
- [P-sum] Tynan Sylvester on The Sims: conversation topics shown in speech bubbles "make no difference" to outcomes, yet players imagine the conversations; a Sims 3 player's story of jealousy and revenge arose from a simulation that modelled only simple attraction: "most of this story is apophenia." — [Sylvester, "The Simulation Dream"](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/the-simulation-dream)

### Inferences
- The planned design (code owns needs, menu and final choice; LLM proposes options and writes thoughts) maps almost one-to-one onto the Sims split between "object advertises" and "agent scores". The LLM can play the role of an extra advertiser that proposes candidate actions with claimed need deltas, while code applies the attenuation curve, distance cost and selection rule. Zubek's "grant the reward only on completion, never on promise" rule is the natural guard against an LLM that over-promises (false advertising produces compulsive loops).
- A minimal robust selection rule drawn from three independent sources (Zubek, Graham, Dill): filter invalid options, keep only the most urgent bucket, drop anything scoring far below the best (e.g. below 10% under the top or outside the top 3 to 5), then weighted-random among the survivors.
- Personality in Sims 1 was implemented in at least three separate places, all cheap: per-agent decay rates, per-agent score multipliers per need, and trait-scaled advertisement strength. Giving one pet a distinctive combination of all three should produce stable, visible preferences without any change to the LLM.
- Forbus's "Skeptical Sims" exercise (compare promised with realised reward and rescale future advertisements) is a simple learned-preference mechanism that Sims 1 lacked. It is the same idea Creatures implemented neurally (section 3) and would let a pet develop likes and dislikes from experience.
- Sims 1 "mood" was itself a need that objects could advertise to, which suggests mood can be treated both as an aggregate output and as something actions can target.

- Exact Sims 1 numbers (decay rates per motive, the actual piecewise curves, the exact N in "random among top N") were not found in an openable primary source. The researcher's recollection is that Sims 1 autonomy chose randomly among roughly the top four advertisements, but this was not verified in this session.
- The leaked Maxis design documents (happiness cutoff per motive; happiness measured over current, daily, weekly and lifetime frames) were visible only as search snippets; the PDFs returned 404.

## 2. Utility AI in general: response curves, considerations, weighted-random selection, inertia, commitment and cooldowns

### Takeaway
Utility AI normalises each input to 0..1, reshapes it with a response curve, combines considerations (multiplying, so any zero vetoes the action), and then selects either the top score or a weighted-random pick from a pruned shortlist. Dithering and repetition are handled by explicit, separate mechanisms: a commitment bonus for the current action, cooldown and runtime considerations, deciding only when the queue is empty, and rank/bucket tiers.

### Cited Findings

**Response curves and normalisation**
- [P-read] "using normalized scores (values that go from 0–1) provide a reasonable starting point"; curve shapes given: linear `U = x/m`; quadratic `U = (x/m)^k` (larger k is steeper; an exponent between 0 and 1 "rotates" the curve so that low x is more urgent); logistic `U = 1/(1 + e^(−x))` with input effectively limited to [−10, 10]; and piecewise linear, hand-tuned from 2D points. "Coming up with the proper function is really more art than science and is usually where you'll spend most of your time." — [Graham, Game AI Pro ch. 9](http://www.gameaipro.com/GameAIPro/GameAIPro_Chapter09_An_Introduction_to_Utility_Theory.pdf)
- [P-read] In the Infinite Axis Utility System (IAUS) as shipped in Guild Wars 2: Heart of Thorns, each action maps to a "decision score evaluator, or DSE"; "Each consideration is essentially a raw numeric input, normalized to the interval [0, 1]. This score is processed through a response curve, which remaps the original number to a final score, again normalized. These scores are then multiplied together to obtain the overall score of the DSE itself." Inputs are normalised with "bookends" (e.g. 0 m to 100 m). — [Lewis, "Choosing Effective Utility-Based Considerations", Game AI Pro 3 ch. 13](http://www.gameaipro.com/GameAIPro3/GameAIPro3_Chapter13_Choosing_Effective_Utility-Based_Considerations.pdf)
- [P-read] Veto by zero: "any consideration can disqualify an entire decision from being selected simply by scoring zero", with early-out so cheap considerations are ordered first. — [Lewis, Game AI Pro 3 ch. 13](http://www.gameaipro.com/GameAIPro3/GameAIPro3_Chapter13_Choosing_Effective_Utility-Based_Considerations.pdf)
- [P-read] "the vast majority of the response curves used in the game are chosen from a small palette of preset curves. Permitting arbitrary curves is immensely powerful but has the substantial drawback of being intimidating". Curve checklist: decide the input and its bookends, slope direction, monotonicity ("generally preferable ... unless some specific behavior is needed"; an upside-down U keeps an enemy at arm's length), and endpoints (should the score reach zero and invalidate, or just deprioritise?). — [Lewis, Game AI Pro 3 ch. 13](http://www.gameaipro.com/GameAIPro3/GameAIPro3_Chapter13_Choosing_Effective_Utility-Based_Considerations.pdf)
- [P-read] Consideration design advice: "Put yourself in the character's shoes ... and think about why you would (or would not!) want to decide to do a particular action. Distill these reasons into concrete metrics." A health-based consideration that dials back a dodge "lends a bit of flavor and personality to the AI characters". — [Lewis, Game AI Pro 3 ch. 13](http://www.gameaipro.com/GameAIPro3/GameAIPro3_Chapter13_Choosing_Effective_Utility-Based_Considerations.pdf)
- [P-read] "One point that cannot be overemphasized is the importance of calculating each option's utility in game, at run time. It is not enough to assign fixed weights to the options a priori." — [Dill, "Dual-Utility Reasoning", Game AI Pro 2 ch. 3](http://www.gameaipro.com/GameAIPro2/GameAIPro2_Chapter03_Dual-Utility_Reasoning.pdf)

**Selection: absolute, relative, dual utility**
- [P-read] Absolute utility (take the highest) "can become predictable since, given a particular situation, it always does the same thing." Relative utility (weight-based random, `P_O = U_O / Σ U_i`) gives variation, but "there is always some chance that an option with very low utility will be selected. This can easily make your AI look stupid." Mitigations: "squaring the weights or ... screening out the lowest weight options". — [Dill, Game AI Pro 2 ch. 3](http://www.gameaipro.com/GameAIPro2/GameAIPro2_Chapter03_Dual-Utility_Reasoning.pdf)
- [P-read] Dual-utility algorithm, verbatim: "1. Eliminate the options with a weight of zero. 2. Find the highest rank category, and eliminate options that don't belong to it. 3. Find the best remaining option (i.e., the one with the highest weight), and eliminate options that are much worse (i.e., much lower weight) than it. 4. Use weight-based random to select from the options that remain." Step 3's cut-off is "some percentage" of the best weight, "configurable on a per-decision basis". "What remains are plausible (not stupid) options." — [Dill, Game AI Pro 2 ch. 3](http://www.gameaipro.com/GameAIPro2/GameAIPro2_Chapter03_Dual-Utility_Reasoning.pdf)
- [P-read] Zoo Tycoon 2 used it for animals and guests: ordinary needs-driven options had rank 0 with varying weights; context-locked behaviour sets (a koala up a tree) had rank around 5 "to ensure that the animal didn't pick some other behavior and pop out of the tree unrealistically"; scripted show behaviours had ranks 98 to 102 coordinated by a small FSM; and "the die behavior had a rank of 1,000,000". — [Dill, Game AI Pro 2 ch. 3](http://www.gameaipro.com/GameAIPro2/GameAIPro2_Chapter03_Dual-Utility_Reasoning.pdf)
- [P-read] Contrast: in Guild Wars 2's IAUS "the best-scoring option determines the action", and Lewis calls always selecting the absolute best-scoring decision "highly recommended" for that combat context. — [Lewis, Game AI Pro 3 ch. 13](http://www.gameaipro.com/GameAIPro3/GameAIPro3_Chapter13_Choosing_Effective_Utility-Based_Considerations.pdf)
- [P-read] Graham on when to use which: "A chess AI should definitely choose the highest scoring move", whereas for The Sims the top pick "can feel very robotic". — [Graham, Game AI Pro ch. 9](http://www.gameaipro.com/GameAIPro/GameAIPro_Chapter09_An_Introduction_to_Utility_Theory.pdf)

**Inertia, commitment, cooldown, repetition**
- [P-read] The problem: "If your AI agent is attempting to decide something every frame, it's possible to run into oscillation issues, especially if you have two things that are scored similarly ... Oscillations in behavior such as this look very bad." Three fixes: (1) "add a weight to any action that you are already currently engaged in. This will cause the AI to tend to remain committed until something truly better comes along"; (2) cooldowns in which "the weighting for remaining in that action is extremely high. This weight can revert at the end of the cooldown period, or it can gradually drop"; (3) "stall making another decision—either for a period of time or until such time as the current action is finished." — [Graham, Game AI Pro ch. 9](http://www.gameaipro.com/GameAIPro/GameAIPro_Chapter09_An_Introduction_to_Utility_Theory.pdf)
- [P-read] IAUS anti-repetition considerations: "a pair of considerations designed to break cycles of repetitive behavior. They are termed runtime and cooldown, respectively. Runtime's job is to guarantee that the decision will stop being chosen after a certain number of repetitions ... a response curve is used to drop the score ... to zero after a short time. This ensures that, even if the decision is otherwise valid, it will not be chosen too many times back-to-back." "Cooldown is a standard timer that forces the decision to score zero (or nearly zero) until a given delay has elapsed. This is useful for avoiding strobing between two otherwise competing decisions and also helps ensure that a single decision is not selected too frequently. The response curve on cooldowns returns very small scores until near the end of the cooldown period, at which time the score spikes back up". — [Lewis, Game AI Pro 3 ch. 13](http://www.gameaipro.com/GameAIPro3/GameAIPro3_Chapter13_Choosing_Effective_Utility-Based_Considerations.pdf)
- [P-read] Against ping-ponging between near-equal decisions: "give a 'commitment bonus' (small score boost factor) to the last-chosen decision ...; add weights to each decision so that their total score could be, say, 3 or 4 instead of just 1; and make judicious use of runtime and cooldown considerations." Caveat: these "do not eliminate the possibility of two decisions oscillating—they simply shift where the scores will land". "The simplest fix ... is to add another consideration to one of the decisions." — [Lewis, Game AI Pro 3 ch. 13](http://www.gameaipro.com/GameAIPro3/GameAIPro3_Chapter13_Choosing_Effective_Utility-Based_Considerations.pdf)
- [P-read] Sequencing for free: "creating a sequence of decisions is as simple as setting up cooldowns, and then having each subsequent decision weighted slightly lower than the prior action. As successive actions are chosen by the AI, they will enter cooldown, leaving the AI to choose the next best thing". — [Lewis, Game AI Pro 3 ch. 13](http://www.gameaipro.com/GameAIPro3/GameAIPro3_Chapter13_Choosing_Effective_Utility-Based_Considerations.pdf)
- [P-read] Tooling: "it is much more effective to build a tool that allows AI developers to manipulate the various inputs for a consideration using simple sliders." — [Lewis, Game AI Pro 3 ch. 13](http://www.gameaipro.com/GameAIPro3/GameAIPro3_Chapter13_Choosing_Effective_Utility-Based_Considerations.pdf)

### Inferences
- For an always-on pet the Sims-style recipe (bucket by urgency, prune, weighted-random) fits better than the Guild Wars 2 recipe (always take the best), because the failure mode to avoid is predictability rather than tactical error. Both camps agree on the same anti-repetition tools.
- Runtime and cooldown expressed as ordinary considerations (multipliers that fall to zero after N consecutive uses and recover over time) are directly reusable as a per-action "satiation" term. This is a code-side cure for an LLM that keeps proposing the same activity: the proposal can still appear on the menu but will score near zero.
- Dill's rank tier is the clean way to separate "must happen now" states (sleeping when exhausted, reacting to the user, being ill) from ordinary idle choices, without tuning weights against each other.
- Multiplicative combination means a single zero vetoes an action, which is a convenient way to enforce hard world constraints on LLM-proposed actions.

### Gaps
- Dave Mark's own materials (the book "Behavioral Mathematics for Game AI" and the GDC AI Summit talks "Improving AI Decision Modeling Through Utility Theory", "Architecture Tricks" and "Building a Better Centaur: AI at Massive Scale") were not opened in this session. The IAUS description above comes from Mike Lewis's chapter, which describes the system he built with Mark.
- The IAUS "compensation factor" (which corrects for scores shrinking as more considerations are multiplied) is not in the Lewis chapter. The researcher's unverified recollection of Mark's GDC formulation is: `modFactor = 1 − 1/numConsiderations; makeUp = (1 − score) × modFactor; final = score + makeUp × score`. Verify against the GDC Vault slides before relying on it.
- No source was found giving concrete magnitudes for commitment bonuses (the recollection that IAUS used a bonus on the order of 25% is unverified) or typical cooldown lengths; these appear to be tuned per game.

## 3. Creatures (Steve Grand): drives, biochemistry, neural network, reinforcement from drive reduction, and how individuality emerged

### Takeaway
Norns had about 1,000 neurons in 9 lobes, a genetically defined biochemistry, and one simple learning rule: reducing a drive releases a Reward chemical that strengthens the synapses behind the recent action, while raising a drive releases Punishment. Because the same action is rewarding or punishing depending on internal state, and because each creature's wiring, chemistry and experience differ, individual preferences emerged without being authored.

### Cited Findings
- [P-read] Scale: "The initial model contains approximately 1,000 neurons, grouped into 9 lobes, and interconnected through roughly 5,000 synapses", all genetically controlled; the genome is "approximately 320 interacting genes, each with several parameters". Up to ten creatures ran on a 1996 PC. — [Grand, Cliff and Malhotra, "Creatures: Artificial Life Autonomous Software Agents for Home Entertainment" (Sussex CSRP 434, 1996)](https://www.sussex.ac.uk/informatics/cogslib/reports/csrp/csrp434.pdf)
- [P-read] Attention bottleneck: stimuli from objects compete by lateral inhibition for the creature's attention; "The creature's gaze ... is fixed on this object, and it becomes the recipient for any actions the creature chooses to take." This limits creatures to "verb object" thought but keeps processing tractable "since the net need only consider one object at a time." — [Grand et al.](https://www.sussex.ac.uk/informatics/cogslib/reports/csrp/csrp434.pdf)
- [P-read] Action set: "The Decision layer comprises only 16 cells, each representing a single possible action, such as 'activate it', 'deactivate it', 'walk west' ... The strongest-firing Decision cell is taken to be the best course of action, and whenever the winner changes, the creature invokes the appropriate action script." Decision cells accumulate "a number of nudges over a short period". — [Grand et al.](https://www.sussex.ac.uk/informatics/cogslib/reports/csrp/csrp434.pdf)
- [P-read] Drives are chemicals: "Each creature maintains a set of chemicals representing 'drives', such as 'the drive to avoid pain', 'the drive to reduce hunger' ... The higher the concentration of each chemical, the more pressing that drive." Objects emit drive raisers and reducers, e.g. a shower may reduce hotness and coldness, decrease tiredness and increase sleepiness. — [Grand et al.](https://www.sussex.ac.uk/informatics/cogslib/reports/csrp/csrp434.pdf)
- [P-read] The learning rule, verbatim: "DriveRaiser → Drive + Punishment" and "DriveReducer + Drive → Reward". "Drive reduction therefore increases the weights of excitatory synapses while drive increase reinforces inhibitory ones. Of course, reducing a non-present drive has no effect, and so the balance of punishment to reward may reverse. Thus, many actions on objects can return a net punishment or a net reward, according to the creature's internal state at the time. Creatures therefore learn to eat when hungry but not when full." "The overall model is behaviorist and based on reinforcement by drive reduction." — [Grand et al.](https://www.sussex.ac.uk/informatics/cogslib/reports/csrp/csrp434.pdf)
- [P-read] Credit assignment for delayed reward: each synapse has a "susceptibility" that "is raised whenever that dendrite is conducting a signal to a cell and that cell is firing (i.e. the connection represents both a 'true' condition and also the current action). It then decays exponentially over time. Synapses are therefore sensitized when they represent relationships between current sensory schemata and the latest action decision, and remain sensitive for a period in order to respond to any share of a more-or-less deferred reward or punishment." — [Grand et al.](https://www.sussex.ac.uk/informatics/cogslib/reports/csrp/csrp434.pdf)
- [P-read] Two-timescale memory: each synapse has a short-term weight (STW) and a long-term weight (LTW) that relax toward each other, LTW more slowly. "The STW therefore reacts strongly to individual reinforcement episodes, while the LTW effectively computes a moving average of many STW disturbances: if a creature meets with situation X and finds that its chosen course of action was undesirable, then it should immediately be strongly disinclined to repeat the action ... However, situation X may not always be as bad as first experience suggests, and so the creature's long-term interpretation should be less sweeping." — [Grand et al.](https://www.sussex.ac.uk/informatics/cogslib/reports/csrp/csrp434.pdf)
- [P-read] Generalisation: Concept Space cells AND together one to four sensory features, so a novel situation ABCD evokes memories of sub-situations such as D or ABD. Example given: "I find myself looking at a big, green thing with staring eyes, which I've never seen before. I remember that going up to things with staring eyes and kissing them is not a good idea, and that hitting big things ... doesn't work well either. So, all in all, I think I'll try something else this time." — [Grand et al.](https://www.sussex.ac.uk/informatics/cogslib/reports/csrp/csrp434.pdf)
- [P-read] Neuron relaxation as damping: each neuron's state relaxes exponentially to a rest state, "since the further the neuron's state gets from equilibrium, the faster it relaxes"; this "keeps the system reasonably stable" and integrates "both the intensity and the frequency of the stimuli." — [Grand et al.](https://www.sussex.ac.uk/informatics/cogslib/reports/csrp/csrp434.pdf)
- [P-read] Biochemistry: chemicals are numbers 0 to 255 with concentrations; emitters, reactions and receptors are genetically defined; reaction rates are concentration-dependent "and therefore exponential over time"; examples include exponential decay (A → nothing) and catalysis. It also implements a metabolism (starch, glucose, glycogen), toxins and a simple immune system, systems "not actually necessary ... yet which would be expected by the general public." — [Grand et al.](https://www.sussex.ac.uk/informatics/cogslib/reports/csrp/csrp434.pdf)
- [P-read] Player feedback uses the same channel: stroking "generates a positive, 'reward' reinforcement signal" and slapping "a negative, 'punishment' reinforcement signal". — [Grand et al.](https://www.sussex.ac.uk/informatics/cogslib/reports/csrp/csrp434.pdf)
- [P-read] Individual differences are genetic and structural: "Genes in our creatures' genomes therefore code for structures such as chemo-receptors, reactions and brain lobes, rather than outward phenomena such as disease-resistance, fearlessness or strength." Offspring show "characteristics identifiably drawn from each parent"; gene linkage allows "linked characteristics ... (for example, temperament with facial type)"; genes have switch-on times so behaviour changes with age (e.g. puberty); lifespan is genetically influenced. — [Grand et al.](https://www.sussex.ac.uk/informatics/cogslib/reports/csrp/csrp434.pdf)
- [P-read] Outcome and caution: behaviour was "a satisfactorily complex and believable sequence of behaviors, surprisingly free from limit cycles or irretrievable collapse into an attractor." Emergent "social" behaviours were seen (cooperating with a ball, "'chase' scenes resulting from 'unrequited love'"), but "it is very difficult to establish how much of this is genuine and how much is conferred by an observer's tendency to anthropomorphism." — [Grand et al.](https://www.sussex.ac.uk/informatics/cogslib/reports/csrp/csrp434.pdf)
- [P-read] Self-assessment: the brain model "is not an ambitious one ... It is also primitively Behaviorist in its reinforcement mechanism." — [Grand et al.](https://www.sussex.ac.uk/informatics/cogslib/reports/csrp/csrp434.pdf)

### Inferences
- The reusable core is small and does not need a neural net: (a) reward equals the actual drop in a currently elevated drive, so the same action is good or bad depending on state; (b) an exponentially decaying eligibility trace on recent (situation, action) pairs spreads delayed reward; (c) each preference has a fast weight and a slow weight, so one bad experience causes strong short-term avoidance but only a modest lasting change. These three give learned, state-dependent, individual preferences as a table of numbers that code can own.
- Individuality in Creatures came from three layers at once: different parameters (genes), different experience (learning), and different momentary state (chemistry). A pet with fixed parameters but real learning would still diverge from any other instance over time.
- The single-object attention bottleneck is a useful design idea for an LLM pet: restricting each decision to "the thing I am attending to plus a verb" keeps the menu small and makes behaviour legible.
- Grand's own caution about anthropomorphism matches Sylvester's apophenia point (section 7): much of the perceived social behaviour was supplied by observers.

### Gaps
- Steve Grand's book "Creation: Life and How to Make It" was not consulted, so his later reflections on why Norns felt alive, and on the limits of the approach, are missing.
- The paper does not list the full set of drives or give numeric decay and learning rates (they live in the genome). Drive lists found online come from fan wikis and were not collected here.
- No controlled evidence was found on how much of Norn individuality players actually perceived as personality rather than noise.

## 4. Tamagotchi and later virtual pets (Petz, Nintendogs, Neopets, Peridot, Finch): what creates attachment and the sense of an individual

### Takeaway
Across virtual pets, attachment is attributed to caregiving with real stakes, dependence in real time, responsiveness, and the feeling that "mine is unique". The best-documented design craft comes from PF.Magic's Petz team: a written personality worksheet per pet, "constrained randomness", many variations per goal, a persistent association memory, and a rule that "every interaction should increase the user's ability to tell a story about the pet". Rigorous research on the "Tamagotchi effect" is thin.

### Cited Findings

**Tamagotchi**
- [S] Mechanics: Hunger and Happy meters, a Discipline (later Training) meter raised by scolding when the pet calls for attention without real need, droppings that cause sickness if not cleaned, age and weight, and a life cycle of Baby, Child, Teenager, Adult (some models add Senior). — [Wikipedia, "Tamagotchi"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tamagotchi)
- [S] Care quality determines the individual: the adult form depends on care during the earlier stages; better care yields an adult that is "smarter, happier, and requires less attention". The original has six adult and two teen characters. — [Wikipedia, "Tamagotchi"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tamagotchi)
- [S] Stakes: in the first two releases a pet could die in less than half a day without adequate care; death can come from poor care, old age or sickness. The original could be paused only by entering the clock-setting screen; later models added a pause, later replaced by a paid babysitting feature. — [Wikipedia, "Tamagotchi"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tamagotchi)
- [S] The "Tamagotchi effect" is defined as the "development of emotional attachment to machines, robots or software agents". Mechanisms listed: caregiving, dependence (the pet dies if neglected), responsiveness, perceived autonomy and human-like traits. Children reportedly called Furbies "kind of alive". The article is flagged by Wikipedia as essay-like and several claims are uncited. — [Wikipedia, "Tamagotchi effect"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tamagotchi_effect)
- [snippet] Academic references surfaced but not read: Bloch and Lemish (1999), "Disposable love: The rise and fall of a virtual pet", New Media & Society; a 2026 DiGRA paper on virtual pets and "affective economies" of care. — [DiGRA paper](https://dl.digra.org/index.php/dl/article/download/3066/3050/3109)

**Petz (PF.Magic: Dogz, Catz, Babyz)**
- [P-sum] "It's more important for the pet to look intelligent than to actually be intelligent." AI "focuses on the result, neglecting timing and performance"; their "Artificial Personality" tools "center around the ability to tightly control timing." — [Frank, Stern and Resner, "Issues of Autonomous Character Design" (CGDC 1997)](https://alumni.media.mit.edu/~benres/verbiage/CDGC97.html)
- [P-sum] Constrained randomness: "A pet with too much random behavior will appear disconnected", while "too little randomness creates a robotic and repetitive pet." "We use what we call 'constrained randomness' to find this balance." Variety comes from "Many variations and alternate scripts for the same goal". "once they see the same animation too many times, the pet ceases to be alive." The team claimed a user "can play with the pet for an hour, and never see the exact same animation frame twice." — [Frank, Stern and Resner](https://alumni.media.mit.edu/~benres/verbiage/CDGC97.html)
- [P-sum] Personality as a design document: "Before actual coding for a pet is started, a personality worksheet is generated", which "drives all subsequent design decisions" so the pet has "a consistent personality towards all objects in its world." — [Frank, Stern and Resner](https://alumni.media.mit.edu/~benres/verbiage/CDGC97.html)
- [P-sum] Ownership and uniqueness: "Every user must be made to feel their pet is unique and special"; "The user only gets a personality template"; "By lavishing or withholding attention or food, the user can change the pet's personality"; "The user and the character form a relationship, where each one learns from the other." — [Frank, Stern and Resner](https://alumni.media.mit.edu/~benres/verbiage/CDGC97.html)
- [P-sum] Story test and responsiveness: "Every interaction should increase the user's ability to tell a story about the pet." "autonomous characters must be able to transition from any state to any other state" quickly, because "If the engine takes a few frames to respond, the effect is lost." Eyelid height and tilt are "extremely powerful tools for communicating the pet's emotional state". — [Frank, Stern and Resner](https://alumni.media.mit.edu/~benres/verbiage/CDGC97.html)
- [P-sum] On death: the team debated that "Immortality cheapens the experience by not making the time precious", but early tests showed users would be scared off by the responsibility, so Petz do not die. — [Frank, Stern and Resner](https://alumni.media.mit.edu/~benres/verbiage/CDGC97.html)
- [P-read] Architecture: "a behavior-based architecture with a model of personality and emotion, all tightly integrated with an expressive realtime-3D-rendered animation system". Behaviours are goals, "each goal having multiple possible plans that can be executed in a non-linear order". "At any decision point, each goal's filter function is queried to compute how important it is for that goal to execute under the current circumstances." "Part of the craft of authoring behaviors is balancing the output of these filter functions; it is easy to accidentally code a behavior to happen far too often or too seldom for believability." — [Stern, "Virtual Babyz: Believable Agents with Narrative Intelligence" (AAAI FS-99-01)](https://cdn.aaai.org/Symposia/Fall/1999/FS-99-01/FS99-01-009.pdf)
- [P-read] Personality expressed on a schedule, not only reactively: goals "can also be spawned deliberately as a need to regularly express the character's particular personality. This is achieved by regularly querying all goals' filter functions in a non-reactive context, each using the character's personality attributes as a basis". A "drama manager" tracks "how often certain dramatically interesting narrative goals have occurred over time, and will deliberately spawn such a goal if the user hasn't experienced one in a while." — [Stern](https://cdn.aaai.org/Symposia/Fall/1999/FS-99-01/FS99-01-009.pdf)
- [P-read] Priority and interruption: "All instantiated goals are sorted in order of priority, with only one goal executing at any one time ... the current active goal can be interrupted and suspended if a higher priority goal is spawned." A narrative "distracted for more than ten or twenty seconds ... may abort". — [Stern](https://cdn.aaai.org/Symposia/Fall/1999/FS-99-01/FS99-01-009.pdf)
- [P-read] Persistent relationship memory: Babyz "have an persistent fuzzy 'association matrix' memory, where they keep track of how positively or negatively they feel towards the user and the other Babyz and objects they encounter. This memory is constantly being updated". First impressions are "a function of how compatible their particular personalities are, how well their first meeting goes ... as well as some randomness." Long-term arcs (a sibling rivalry whose pranks escalate over time) are "a simple persistent episodic memory, which is queried by the drama manager to decide what short-term prank narrative goals to spawn next". — [Stern](https://cdn.aaai.org/Symposia/Fall/1999/FS-99-01/FS99-01-009.pdf)
- [P-read] Illusion of learning: "All behaviors are pre-authored, with the user's interaction unlocking them over time, to create the illusion that the Babyz are learning." Development stages are "a set of simple persistent variables" that modify "how all other goals and plans are performed." — [Stern](https://cdn.aaai.org/Symposia/Fall/1999/FS-99-01/FS99-01-009.pdf)
- [P-read] Emergent stories were unplanned: with multiple pets on screen "the interplay between the variety of rich personalities gave rise to many dramatic situations. Small 'stories' seemed to emerge ... Without explicitly building narrative into the system". Stern attributes part of this to the "Eliza effect". Petz sold "over 2 million copies ... as of 1998" and users "posted hundreds of messages ... describing their experiences and relationships with their individual Petz." — [Stern](https://cdn.aaai.org/Symposia/Fall/1999/FS-99-01/FS99-01-009.pdf)

**Nintendogs**
- [snippet] In Nintendo's Iwata Asks interviews, producer Hideki Konno says he wanted to bring out differences of personality (quiet and lively, cautious and friendly, particular likes and dislikes) and that the team kept adjusting this until the end of development; Miyamoto argues that because dogs of the same breed differ, players get a much stronger feeling of which puppy is theirs. Pages were not opened; wording is paraphrase from search results. — [Iwata Asks: nintendogs + cats](https://iwataasks.nintendo.com/interviews/3ds/how-nintendo-3ds-made/3/1)

**Peridot (Niantic)**
- [snippet] Niantic announced on 15 November 2023 that Peridot uses generative AI to drive its creatures' reactions: after the AR system recognises a real-world object, the backend asks a Llama model how a small fluffy creature might respond to what it found, and the response is mapped onto existing animations. Meta's case study says Niantic integrated Llama 2 in November 2023. The official post now redirects and returned 404, so details are from search snippets. — [Niantic announcement (now redirecting)](https://nianticlabs.com/news/peridot-generative-ai); [Meta AI blog](https://ai.meta.com/blog/niantic-peridot-llama/)

### Inferences
- The Petz "personality worksheet" is the 1997 equivalent of a character sheet in a system prompt, with the important difference that it drove code-side decisions (filter functions) and not just dialogue. For an LLM pet, the worksheet should parameterise the scoring code (decay rates, weights, liked and disliked objects) as well as the prompt.
- Two Petz mechanisms target the "generic, repetitive LLM" problem directly: a scheduled, non-reactive pass whose only job is to express personality when nothing else is happening, and a drama manager that tracks how long it has been since something interesting happened and spawns it. Both are code-side and cheap.
- A small persistent association matrix (valence toward the user and each object, updated on every interaction, with initial values partly random) is a proven way to get durable likes, dislikes and grudges. It pairs naturally with the Creatures learning rule.
- Tamagotchi's individuality comes from path dependence: the same starting creature becomes a different adult depending on accumulated care. Irreversible, history-dependent branching is a cheap way to make "this one" feel distinct.
- Petz chose no death and Tamagotchi chose fast death; both report strong attachment. The common factor appears to be that the user's care visibly changes the creature, not mortality itself.
- Peridot is the nearest shipped precedent to the planned architecture: perception and animation selection in code, with the LLM proposing a reaction.

### Gaps
- No rigorous empirical study measuring which virtual-pet mechanics cause attachment was opened. The Wikipedia "Tamagotchi effect" article is weak (essay-flagged, partly uncited), and the Bloch and Lemish paper and Sherry Turkle's work were not read.
- No designer statements from Aki Maita or Akihiro Yokoi about why Tamagotchi creates attachment were found.
- Nothing was found on Neopets or Finch (the self-care pet app) mechanics from primary sources.
- Exact Tamagotchi numbers (meter sizes, decay intervals, care-mistake thresholds for each evolution) are documented only on fan wikis, which were not collected.
- Peridot's actual prompt inputs (whether the Dot's traits, mood or history are passed to the model) could not be confirmed.
- The 1998 "Virtual Petz" Agents paper with the full emotion model was not read in full.

## 5. Dwarf Fortress and RimWorld: personality, needs, thoughts and memories that modify mood, mental breaks, and emergent stories

### Takeaway
Both games drive mood from many small, labelled, time-limited "thoughts" rather than from one state variable. RimWorld sums thought values onto a base to get a target that actual mood chases at a capped rate, with break thresholds at 35%, 20% and 5%. Dwarf Fortress keeps eight short-term and eight long-term memory slots, promotes strong memories after a year, has dwarves relive them, and lets relived memories permanently change personality. Named, legible causes are what turn mood arithmetic into stories.

### Cited Findings

**RimWorld mood arithmetic**
- [W] Mood target = base mood + net sum of all current thoughts. Base mood depends on difficulty: 42 (Peaceful, Community Builder), 37 (Adventure Story), 32 (Strive to Survive), 27 (Blood and Dust), 22 (Losing is Fun). Example: Peaceful with Low expectations (+18) and Hungry (−6) gives 42 + 18 − 6 = 54%. — [RimWorld Wiki, "Mood"](https://rimworldwiki.com/wiki/Mood)
- [W] Mood does not jump: it rises toward the target at a maximum of +12 per in-game hour and falls at a maximum of −8 per in-game hour. While a colonist is asleep or unconscious the mood bar holds and break risk is paused. — [RimWorld Wiki, "Mood"](https://rimworldwiki.com/wiki/Mood)
- [W] Mental break thresholds for a normal colonist: minor break risk below 35%, major below 20%, extreme below 5%; traits shift these thresholds. High mood can trigger inspirations. — [RimWorld Wiki, "Mood"](https://rimworldwiki.com/wiki/Mood)
- [W] Thought types: memory thoughts (one-off events with a fixed duration in days, a stack limit and a stacking multiplier), situational thoughts (active while a condition holds: cold −4/−8/−12/−16, darkness −5, tattered apparel −5), and expectations (permanent while applicable: extremely low +30, very low +24, low +18, moderate +12, high +6; noble −6, royal −12). — [RimWorld Wiki, "Thoughts"](https://rimworldwiki.com/wiki/Thoughts)
- [W] Example memory thoughts (mood, duration in days, stack limit, stack multiplier): ate fine meal +5, 1, 1; ate lavish meal +12, 1, 1 (replaces fine meal); ate without table −3, 1, 1; colonist died −3, 6, 5, 0.75; friend died −10, 20, 5, 0.75; son died −20, 30, 10, 0.75; insulted −5, 2, 10, 0.9; kind words +5, 2, 10, 0.9; got some lovin' +8, 3, 10, 0.6; attended party +8, 10, 10, 0.75; broken up with −15, 25, 2, 0.75. — [RimWorld Wiki, "Thoughts"](https://rimworldwiki.com/wiki/Thoughts)
- [W] Traits rewrite the thought table rather than adding a separate personality system: nullifying traits remove a thought (Psychopath and Bloodlust null many death thoughts), required traits gate one ("Killed someone" +12 for 4 days applies only to Bloodlust pawns), and some flip the sign (raw cannibalism is −20 normally, +20 for a Cannibal). — [RimWorld Wiki, "Thoughts"](https://rimworldwiki.com/wiki/Thoughts)

**RimWorld design intent (Tynan Sylvester)**
- [S] From an auto-generated transcript of his GDC 2017 talk: "RimWorld is a story generator, the player interacts with it, and stories emerge from that interaction." His feature test is "Does this enrich the emotional journeys of the characters?", used to cut smelting chains, fluid dynamics and animal breeding. — [Transcript notes of "RimWorld: Contrarian, Ridiculous, and Impossible Game Design Methods"](https://readray.ai/notes/1141351); talk listed at [Game Developer](https://gamedeveloper.com/design/video-how-i-rimworld-i-found-success-through-ridiculous-contrarian-design)
- [S] Memory as story glue: colonists remember deaths, marriages, divorces and failed surgeries; a thought such as "Botched my surgery" lets players connect past events to the present. Colonists are not fully controlled (they may ignore orders, binge, sleep), which lets players watch events rather than feel wholly responsible. Expectation bonuses at low wealth let a wrecked colony keep functioning during recovery. — [Transcript notes](https://readray.ai/notes/1141351)
- [S] "Elastic failure": failure should stretch into deeper dramatic states "instead of failure being a brick wall that you run into and shatter on"; raiders who have kidnapped or stolen enough leave, so the story continues. — [Transcript notes](https://readray.ai/notes/1141351)

**Dwarf Fortress memory and stress**
- [W] Each dwarf has 8 short-term and 8 long-term memory slots plus unlimited core memories. Memories are grouped by category and only the strongest emotion per category keeps a slot, "so similar memories don't crowd out other trauma". A new thought in an existing category keeps the stronger of the two; in a new category it takes an empty slot or overwrites the weakest short-term memory. — [Dwarf Fortress Wiki, "Memory (thought)"](https://dwarffortresswiki.org/index.php/Memory_(thought))
- [W] Promotion: a short-term memory is promoted to long-term after one year in its slot (subject to strength comparisons with what is already there). During that year it applies an additional stress change. — [Dwarf Fortress Wiki, "Memory (thought)"](https://dwarffortresswiki.org/index.php/Memory_(thought))
- [W] Reliving: long-term memories "periodically resurface" until overwritten. In a sample of 300 dwarves the median number of revisited memories was 9, and 83% had revisited 8 or more. — [Dwarf Fortress Wiki, "Memory (thought)"](https://dwarffortresswiki.org/index.php/Memory_(thought))
- [W] Personality change: each time a long-term memory is relived there is a 1 in 3 chance it moves to core memory (if its category is eligible). Core memories cause permanent changes to personality traits and values, displayed to the player with direction, cause and year. Most eligible memories are negative (witnessing death, trauma); positive ones include new romance, marriage and becoming a parent. — [Dwarf Fortress Wiki, "Memory (thought)"](https://dwarffortresswiki.org/index.php/Memory_(thought))
- [snippet] Stress is a running point total that unhappy thoughts raise and happy thoughts lower, ranging from −50,000 to 120,000, with labels at +25,000 (stressed), +50,000 (haggard) and +100,000 (harrowed). Three facets govern it: bravery (how fast stress accumulates), stress vulnerability (how much can be borne before breaking) and anxiety (how fast it dissipates). Personality facets run 0 to 100, and values between 40 and 60 are not mentioned in the dwarf's profile. The Stress page returned HTTP 429, so these figures are from search snippets of the wiki. — [Dwarf Fortress Wiki, "Stress"](https://dwarffortresswiki.org/Stress); [Dwarf Fortress Wiki, "Personality trait"](https://dwarffortresswiki.org/index.php/Personality_Trait)
- [S] The memory system was announced by the developers as giving dwarves "the ability to form and dwell on memories". — [Game Developer](https://gamedeveloper.com/game-platforms/-i-dwarf-fortress-i-is-giving-its-dwarves-the-ability-to-form-and-dwell-on-memories)

**Dwarf Fortress design intent (Tarn Adams)**
- [P-read] Four simulation principles: "Don't Overplan Your Model" ("get something running as soon as you can ... build it up through iteration"); "Break Down and Understand the System" (model the basic elements and their interactions, and "certain problems solve themselves"); "Don't Overcomplicate" ("There's no reason to have 50 variables modeling one aspect of a game's behavior if they don't all have a meaningful impact on the game. Operate at the level of what the player sees or one layer below. Don't get carried away and create a lot of worthless noise"); and "Base Your Model on Real-World Analogs". — [Adams, "Simulation Principles from Dwarf Fortress", Game AI Pro 2 ch. 41](http://www.gameaipro.com/GameAIPro2/GameAIPro2_Chapter41_Simulation_Principles_from_Dwarf_Fortress.pdf)
- [P-read] "Allow the individual elements to combine, producing results which can be varied, surprising, and yet still responsive to your adjustments. By keeping it simple, you can avoid black box simulations and ultimately stay in control of your game." — [Adams, Game AI Pro 2 ch. 41](http://www.gameaipro.com/GameAIPro2/GameAIPro2_Chapter41_Simulation_Principles_from_Dwarf_Fortress.pdf)
- [snippet] In his essay on characterisation, Adams writes that "using a few distinct traits would not work in Dwarf Fortress because characters in the game need to respond to a diverse set of conditions", that players often read in an interior life for characters well beyond what the game was modelling, and that some elements "like names have no mechanical purpose and are used purely as storytelling aids". The PDF returned 404, so this is from search snippets. — [Adams, "Characterization and Emergent Narrative in Dwarf Fortress"](https://wileywiggins.com//documents/dorf/Adams%20-%20Characterization%20and%20Emergent%20Narrative%20in%20Dwarf%20F.pdf)
- [S] PC Gamer headline quote attributed to Adams on dwarves being "more human than human": "They're allowed to embody bigger emotions. They're allowed to make bigger mistakes. They're allowed to do anything". The article body could not be retrieved. — [PC Gamer](https://www.pcgamer.com/games/sim/dwarf-fortress-dwarves-are-more-human-than-human-creator-says-theyre-allowed-to-embody-bigger-emotions-theyre-allowed-to-make-bigger-mistakes-theyre-allowed-to-do-anything)

### Inferences
- A directly reusable mood model: `target = base + Σ(active thought values)`; `mood` moves toward `target` at a capped rate, faster upward than downward in RimWorld's case (+12 versus −8 per hour); thresholds trigger qualitatively different behaviour modes. Each thought is a record of (label, value, expiry, stack limit, stack multiplier). The rate cap gives emotional inertia, and expiry gives forgetting.
- The thought list doubles as explanation. Because every mood contribution has a human-readable label and cause, it can be shown to the user and fed to the LLM as the grounded reason for the pet's current feelings, which keeps generated "thoughts" tied to simulated facts instead of generic sentiment.
- Dwarf Fortress adds what RimWorld lacks: consolidation and character change. A fixed number of slots, one per category, strongest wins, promotion after a long interval, periodic reliving, and a chance that reliving permanently nudges a trait. This is a bounded-size long-term memory with built-in salience ranking, well suited to an always-on agent with a limited context window.
- Traits implemented as edits to the thought table (nullify, gate, flip sign) make personality show up as different reactions to the same event, which observers read as individuality more readily than different idle animations.
- Stack limits with multipliers below 1 are a guard against one repeated event dominating mood, the affective counterpart of cooldowns in action selection.
- Break thresholds give the pet states in which it does not behave sensibly. Wright, Sylvester and Adams all treat imperfect, sometimes self-defeating behaviour as a source of stories.

### Gaps
- RimWorld's exact stacking formula was not on the page read. The researcher's unverified recollection is that the nth stacked copy of a memory contributes its value times multiplier^(n−1).
- RimWorld's per-trait break-threshold shifts, inspiration thresholds, and how a specific mental break is chosen from the eligible list (believed to be weighted random by commonality) were not verified.
- The Dwarf Fortress Stress and Needs pages could not be opened (rate limited). Stress numbers above are snippet-level, and the needs and focus system (unmet needs lowering focus and skill performance) is not documented here. How personality facets numerically scale the stress from each emotion is also unverified.
- Tarn Adams's essay "Characterization and Emergent Narrative in Dwarf Fortress" and his chapter in "Procedural Storytelling in Game Design" (2019) were not read in full.
- Sylvester's book "Designing Games" was not consulted. The Rock Paper Shotgun piece "How RimWorld Generates Great Stories" could not be fetched. The GDC talk quotes come from an auto-generated transcript with known transcription errors.
- All RimWorld and Dwarf Fortress numbers come from fan wikis and may vary by game version and DLC.

## 6. Black & White's creature and other learning-by-feedback pets: how reward and punishment shaped preferences

### Takeaway
Black & White's creature combined a belief-desire-intention structure with perceptrons for desires and decision trees for "opinions", trained by the player's strokes and slaps and by watching the player. Creatures used the same stroke and slap inputs but routed them through the general drive-reduction learning rule. In both, feedback altered the weights behind choices rather than scripting behaviour, so each player's creature diverged.

### Cited Findings
- [S] Architecture: Lionhead used Bratman's belief-desire-intention model. Beliefs are lists of data about world objects; desires are goals represented as simplified perceptrons; opinions describe ways to satisfy a desire using decision trees. For each desire the creature selects the belief with the best opinion, forming its intention. — [Wikipedia, "Black & White (video game)"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black_%26_White_(video_game))
- [S] Feedback: teaching uses reinforcement learning; unwanted behaviour is discouraged with a slap and approved behaviour rewarded with a stroke; the creature remembers how it was treated and gradually changes its behaviour. Three leashes exist: one makes the creature pay attention to demonstrated actions (learning by observation), the others bias it toward benevolent or malevolent behaviour. — [Wikipedia, "Black & White (video game)"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black_%26_White_(video_game))
- [S] Species differ in learning rate and strength (apes learn well but are weak; tigers are strong but learn slowly), and the creature has its own alignment separate from the player's, shown in its appearance. — [Wikipedia, "Black & White (video game)"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black_%26_White_(video_game))
- [P-sum] Peter Molyneux's postmortem: Richard Evans had to consider "what learning was, how practice works, and how the reinforcement of ideas comes about", and built a creature that would "appear to live and learn like, say, a clever puppy". "Once he starts learning, he forms his own personality as he goes, and no two players will ever have the same Creature." The AI "became something that surprised even him with its flexibility and power." — [Molyneux, "Postmortem: Lionhead Studios' Black & White", Game Developer](https://gamedeveloper.com/design/postmortem-lionhead-studios-i-black-white-i-)
- [P-sum] Design constraints from the same postmortem: complexity was "kept to a minimum to keep him fast"; human creatures were dropped because of the discipline mechanic ("We certainly couldn't have the player slapping a child or a woman or, really, even a grown man"); and players "wouldn't expect a turtle to learn as quickly as a man". — [Molyneux postmortem](https://gamedeveloper.com/design/postmortem-lionhead-studios-i-black-white-i-)
- [S] The game was listed in the 2003 Guinness World Records for the "most intelligent being in a game"; a later GameSpy re-review called it the most overrated game of all time, citing poor use of the creature. — [Wikipedia, "Black & White (video game)"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black_%26_White_(video_game))
- [P-read] Creatures: the hand cursor can stroke a creature "(which generates a positive, 'reward' reinforcement signal)" or slap it "(to generate a negative, 'punishment' reinforcement signal)". These feed the same Reward and Punishment chemicals that drive changes produce, strengthening or inhibiting whichever (situation, action) synapses are still "susceptible". — [Grand et al.](https://www.sussex.ac.uk/informatics/cogslib/reports/csrp/csrp434.pdf)
- [P-sum] Petz: "By lavishing or withholding attention or food, the user can change the pet's personality", and users can "teach them tricks"; the team "had to continually tweak the petting algorithm", often "reducing the sensitivity of petting". — [Frank, Stern and Resner](https://alumni.media.mit.edu/~benres/verbiage/CDGC97.html)
- [P-read] Babyz deliberately faked learning: behaviours are pre-authored and unlocked by interaction "to create the illusion that the Babyz are learning", while real adaptation lives in the association matrix. — [Stern](https://cdn.aaai.org/Symposia/Fall/1999/FS-99-01/FS99-01-009.pdf)

### Inferences
- Two families of mechanism exist. One is real learning of weights from feedback (Creatures, Black & White); the other is authored progression unlocked by feedback plus a small valence memory (Petz, Babyz). The second is far cheaper and was commercially successful, so "illusion of learning plus real preference memory" is a legitimate target.
- The shared hard problem is credit assignment: which recent action does a stroke or slap refer to? Creatures solved it with decaying per-synapse susceptibility. An LLM pet needs an equivalent explicit rule, for example applying feedback to the current or most recent action with a weight that decays over a few minutes.
- Black & White's framing that the creature has its own alignment separate from the player's supports the user's goal of a pet that is an individual: feedback biases preferences but the creature still chooses.
- Species-level differences in learning rate show that how fast a creature takes feedback is itself a personality parameter.

### Gaps
- Richard Evans's own technical account ("Varieties of Learning" in AI Game Programming Wisdom, 2002) is not online and was not read. The BDI, perceptron and decision-tree description therefore rests on Wikipedia's summary of it. The actual desire formula, the attributes the decision trees split on, and how feedback magnitude was computed are unverified.
- A University of Rochester student paper often cited for the internals (Wexler, "Artificial Intelligence in Games: A look at the smarts behind Lionhead Studio's Black and White") returned HTTP 403.
- The widely repeated anecdotes of the creature mislearning (for example associating a slap with the wrong recent action) were not found in a reliable source in this session. One low-quality mirror attributed an anecdote about the creature being sick on a villager to Evans via Eurogamer; this was not verified.
- Nothing rigorous was found on how much players' feedback actually changed creature behaviour in practice.

## 7. What designers say about randomness, failure and constraints in making stories emerge

### Takeaway
The consistent message is that stories come from the observer's interpretation of simple, legible, emotionally loaded events, not from simulation depth; that some bounded randomness is necessary but must stay within plausible limits; and that failure and imperfect characters are a feature, provided failure bends rather than ends the story.

### Cited Findings

**Stories are co-authored by the observer**
- [P-sum] The Player Model Principle: "the whole value of a game is in the mental model of itself it projects into the player's mind"; "anything in the Game Model that doesn't copy into the Player Model is worthless." Over-complex simulation becomes a "gigantic hairball of complexity" where "it becomes very hard for players to understand cause and effect in the system, so those stories end up buried and unobserved." — [Sylvester, "The Simulation Dream"](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/the-simulation-dream)
- [P-sum] Apophenia, "seeing meaningful patterns in random or meaningless data": "we can't simulate the emotional core of a good story on silicon"; "the simulation is a co-author of stories with the player." Ways to invite it: borrow familiar archetypes, let players project themselves, put "human values ... at stake" ("life/death, alone/together, wealth/poverty"), and express primal emotions. — [Sylvester, "The Simulation Dream"](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/the-simulation-dream)
- [P-sum] "Choose the minimum representation that supports the kinds of stories you want to generate" and "lean on the simple side". "Hair complexity" (detail that affects nothing else) is cheap and valuable: in Dwarf Fortress "each dwarf has an appearance. These appearances do nothing, but help players form mental images." Story-richness is "the percentage of interactions in a game that are interesting to the player", so a game must be a "narratively condensed, intensified version" of its subject. — [Sylvester, "The Simulation Dream"](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/the-simulation-dream)
- [S] "the human mind is really an overactive system for matching patterns"; withholding detail (no visible faces when a doctor treats his wife) lets players imagine the emotional content. — [Transcript notes of Sylvester's GDC 2017 talk](https://readray.ai/notes/1141351)
- [S] Matt Brown (Sims 2): "Consistency leads to player storytelling and ascription of personality"; hidden complexity "is usually interpreted as random behavior by the player". — [Nelson's talk notes](https://www.kmjn.org/notes/sims2_ai.html)
- [P-read] Zubek: without feedback such as thought bubbles the player "is likely to build an entirely different mental model of what the agent is doing." — [Zubek](https://robert.zubek.net/publications/Needs-based-AI-draft.pdf)
- [P-read] Grand et al. and Stern both concede that observers supply part of the effect: "an observer's tendency to anthropomorphism" and the "Eliza effect". — [Grand et al.](https://www.sussex.ac.uk/informatics/cogslib/reports/csrp/csrp434.pdf); [Stern](https://cdn.aaai.org/Symposia/Fall/1999/FS-99-01/FS99-01-009.pdf)

**Randomness: necessary, but bounded**
- [P-read] Determinism reads as robotic: weighted-random avoids "having the agent look unpleasantly deterministic" (Zubek); the best action every time "can feel very robotic" (Graham); absolute utility "can become predictable" (Dill). — [Zubek](https://robert.zubek.net/publications/Needs-based-AI-draft.pdf); [Graham](http://www.gameaipro.com/GameAIPro/GameAIPro_Chapter09_An_Introduction_to_Utility_Theory.pdf); [Dill](http://www.gameaipro.com/GameAIPro2/GameAIPro2_Chapter03_Dual-Utility_Reasoning.pdf)
- [P-read] Unbounded randomness reads as stupid: "every now and then, they'll choose something utterly stupid" (Graham); a low-utility pick "can easily make your AI look stupid" (Dill). Hence prune first, then randomise among "plausible (not stupid) options". — [Graham](http://www.gameaipro.com/GameAIPro/GameAIPro_Chapter09_An_Introduction_to_Utility_Theory.pdf); [Dill](http://www.gameaipro.com/GameAIPro2/GameAIPro2_Chapter03_Dual-Utility_Reasoning.pdf)
- [P-sum] Petz "constrained randomness": too much and the pet "will appear disconnected"; too little "creates a robotic and repetitive pet". — [Frank, Stern and Resner](https://alumni.media.mit.edu/~benres/verbiage/CDGC97.html)
- [S] Sims 2: a random-number generator replaced an elaborate urinal-choice model that players could not read; some randomness "helps avoid brittleness" and exploitable loops. — [Nelson's talk notes](https://www.kmjn.org/notes/sims2_ai.html)
- [P-read] Randomness in initial conditions: Babyz' first impressions of each other include "some randomness", which then compounds into long-running rivalries. — [Stern](https://cdn.aaai.org/Symposia/Fall/1999/FS-99-01/FS99-01-009.pdf)
- [P-sum] Counter-example: for Black & White's creature the team "avoided random elements"; variety was meant to come from learning. — [Molyneux postmortem](https://gamedeveloper.com/design/postmortem-lionhead-studios-i-black-white-i-)

**Failure and imperfection**
- [S] Will Wright (reported): early Sims autonomy "was too good"; "Almost anything the player did was worse than the Sims running on autopilot", so it was reduced. — [PC Gamer](https://www.pcgamer.com/games/the-sims/will-wright-says-the-original-sims-ai-was-actually-too-good-almost-anything-the-player-did-was-worse-than-the-sims-running-on-autopilot)
- [P-read] Failures are content: lazy action chaining "makes interesting failures easier", e.g. a stove that can "randomly push a 'create a kitchen fire' action"; a failed cooking chain can leave a "burned food" object to clean up. — [Zubek](https://robert.zubek.net/publications/Needs-based-AI-draft.pdf)
- [S] Sylvester's "elastic failure": failure should stretch into deeper dramatic states rather than be "a brick wall"; RimWorld deliberately breaks the assumption that skill is rewarded proportionally, because fiction's protagonists suffer disproportionate setbacks. — [Transcript notes](https://readray.ai/notes/1141351)
- [P-sum] Petz: a dog that struggles with a task can be more appealing than one that solves it instantly, even though the latter has "a superior AI engine." — [Frank, Stern and Resner](https://alumni.media.mit.edu/~benres/verbiage/CDGC97.html)
- [S] Tarn Adams (headline quote): dwarves are "allowed to make bigger mistakes". — [PC Gamer](https://www.pcgamer.com/games/sim/dwarf-fortress-dwarves-are-more-human-than-human-creator-says-theyre-allowed-to-embody-bigger-emotions-theyre-allowed-to-make-bigger-mistakes-theyre-allowed-to-do-anything)

**Constraints and simplicity**
- [P-read] Adams: "Don't Overcomplicate ... Operate at the level of what the player sees or one layer below"; simple interacting elements give results "varied, surprising, and yet still responsive to your adjustments", avoiding "black box simulations". — [Adams, Game AI Pro 2 ch. 41](http://www.gameaipro.com/GameAIPro2/GameAIPro2_Chapter41_Simulation_Principles_from_Dwarf_Fortress.pdf)
- [P-read] Constraints as enablers in Creatures: attending to one object at a time and choosing among only 16 actions kept the system computable and still gave "a satisfactorily complex and believable sequence of behaviors". — [Grand et al.](https://www.sussex.ac.uk/informatics/cogslib/reports/csrp/csrp434.pdf)
- [P-read] Balance is the hard part, not capability: "it is easy to accidentally code a behavior to happen far too often or too seldom for believability" (Stern); balanced object sets where characters "interact with them in natural-looking patterns" are harder than addictive ones (Forbus). — [Stern](https://cdn.aaai.org/Symposia/Fall/1999/FS-99-01/FS99-01-009.pdf); [Forbus](https://qrg.northwestern.edu/papers/Files/Programming_Objects_in_The_Sims.pdf)
- [P-sum] Petz story test: "Every interaction should increase the user's ability to tell a story about the pet." — [Frank, Stern and Resner](https://alumni.media.mit.edu/~benres/verbiage/CDGC97.html)

### Inferences
- The precedents converge on a prescription that matches the planned architecture: keep the simulated state small and legible, make causes visible, randomise only among plausible options, and let both the human observer and the LLM narrator supply the meaning. The LLM's natural role is the labelled "thought bubble" layer that turns simple state changes into readable interior life; its weakness (generic, repetitive output) is addressed by grounding it in specific, named simulation facts and by code-side cooldowns.
- "Too competent is boring" has a direct analogue: an LLM pet that always makes the sensible, well-balanced choice will read as a generic assistant. Suboptimal but in-character choices, visible struggle, occasional failure and bad moods are what the cited designers credit for stories.
- Legibility is as important as the mechanism. Several sources independently warn that hidden sophistication reads as noise, so each autonomous choice should be attributable to a visible need, mood contribution or stated preference.
- There is a real disagreement on randomness. Black & White avoided it in favour of learning-driven variety, while The Sims, Petz and the utility-AI authors embrace bounded randomness. The two are compatible: learned preferences shift the weights and bounded randomness samples from them.

### Gaps
- Sylvester's "Designing Games" (the book-length treatment of apophenia and emergent story) and Adams's long-form interviews were not read; several Adams and Wright quotes are headline-level or secondary.
- None of these sources offers quantitative evidence (for example retention or attachment measurements) that a given amount of randomness or failure improves perceived aliveness; the claims are practitioner judgement.
- No source was found that evaluates these mechanisms specifically for LLM-driven characters. Applying them to an LLM pet is inference from the scripted and utility-AI era, with Peridot (2023) as the only shipped LLM-reaction precedent identified, and its internals unconfirmed.
